# Ocean accounts as compilation infrastructure for a blue economy index

## Abstract

Indonesia is statutorily obliged to score every province annually on a 44-indicator blue economy
composite compiled, on its own registration, as sectoral statistics with no primary collection.
Indonesia's ocean accounts are assessed here as compilation infrastructure for that index rather
than as an alternative indicator source.
Here, the analysis demonstrates that what accounting offers such an index is a production
process, being a standing classification, a balancing identity, a re-aggregable spatial basis
and a documented revision rule, rather than a stock of substitutable values.
Reading the register's verbatim constituent data items rather than its indicator names corrects
prior assessments: three condition indicators ask for the share of provincial area within a stated
cover band, a tabulation of extent by class rather than the index against reference condition they
found missing. Supporting that claim, the account types
Indonesia has compiled, at one pilot site, carry about 7.6% of the composite's
published weight, whilst those bearing on roughly 19% remain methodology under development, both
shares contingent on an undocumented ambiguity in how the weights are expressed. What an accounting
system can carry for a statutory composite is now a general question.


# 1. Introduction

Composite indices have become a standard instrument of ocean governance, converting heterogeneous
marine statistics into a single number that ministries can target, budget against and report. Their
credibility rests less on indicator design than on the production system beneath them, since an index
can only be as traceable as the statistics it consumes. That dependency is rarely examined, in part
because indices are usually assessed on what they measure rather than on how their inputs are made.

Composite indices of ocean condition are themselves an established form, the Ocean Health Index scoring
every coastal country against ten goals for a coupled human–ocean system (Halpern et al., 2012), and the
methodological difficulties they meet are documented rather than novel, the standard reference treating
imputation, weighting and aggregation as the three choices that most move a published ranking
(Nardo et al., 2008). The Indonesian case therefore belongs to a recognised class of measurement problem
and not to an idiosyncratic one.

Ocean accounting has developed as the candidate infrastructure for that problem. The System of
Environmental-Economic Accounting Ecosystem Accounting framework, adopted by the UN Statistical
Commission in 2021 as an international statistical standard, supplies the extent, condition, service
and asset structures on which such accounts are built (United Nations et al., 2024), and a complete
national sequence has been specified in detail (IDEEA Group, 2021). Ocean accounts have been argued
to belong in the national statistical apparatus rather than in analysis, on the grounds that recurrent
policy reporting requires a production system and not a study (Fenichel et al., 2020), and
sub-national compilation across extent, service flows and ocean-economy production has been
demonstrated operationally (Gacutan et al., 2022). In Indonesia, the spatial basis such accounts
require has been tested empirically, with grid size and designation rule shown to change the extent
estimate materially (Rahayu et al., 2024). What none of that work establishes is whether an account
compiled to those specifications can carry the reporting load of a statutory index, which is a
question about production capacity rather than about accounting method.

Indonesia is where the policy demand is most explicit. Its ocean policy lineage runs from the 2017
Indonesian Ocean Policy through a development framework (BAPPENAS, 2021) to a blue economy roadmap
(BAPPENAS, 2024), and a recent review of that lineage names limited data support among the obstacles to
implementation (Wuwung et al., 2024). Quantitative work on Indonesian blue economy indicators remains
thin, and Rinaldi et al. (2025) construct their own sub-national index for Sumatera on a human
development index template and regress it on regional product, which is the closest existing treatment
and is not an analysis of the official index. What has not been examined is the production system
beneath that index itself.

The Indonesia Blue Economy Index (IBEI) is an official national development indicator under
presidential regulation Perpres 12/2025, with provincial targets for 2045 assigned by joint circular
of the Ministry of Home Affairs and the Ministry of National Development Planning (BAPPENAS) for
carriage in long-term development plans. A province cannot decline to be scored, and BAPPENAS cannot
decline to score it. Against that recurring obligation, the compilation activity is registered in
Indonesia's statistical activity system (SIRUSA kegiatan 94936) as sectoral statistics gathered by
secondary collection from the publications of the Ministry of Marine Affairs and Fisheries (MMAF,
Indonesian KKP) and of Statistics Indonesia (BPS), so the index conducts no primary collection of its
own and inherits whatever those agencies publish, at whatever resolution they publish it. The
traceability defects that follow from compiling a 44-indicator provincial composite entirely from
secondary publications are legible in the index's own documentation and are documented in Section 2.

Here, this study assesses Indonesia's ocean accounts as compilation infrastructure for an index the
state is already obliged to produce, rather than as an alternative source of indicator values. The
assessment is built on the IBEI's own published documentation, being the 2025 technical handbook
(Juknis) and the December 2025 revision of its report, on the primary reports of the Indonesian
account pilots, and on the international accounting standard, with every claim tagged for maturity.
It contributes a documented account of what makes provincial compilation hard, a three-tier indicator
mapping scored against the register's verbatim constituent data items rather than its indicator names,
a quantification of where in the index the account-relevant weight sits, and a register of the
conditions precedent for continued supply. Two boundaries hold throughout the assessment. Account
compilation has its own statutory and analytical purposes under MMAF's mandate, and supplying an index
is one downstream use among several, so nothing here proposes replacing the IBEI or subordinating
either framework, the relationship examined being statistical infrastructure feeding a policy
instrument. The object of study is bounded to the 44-indicator edition of the index, and every
Indonesian ocean account output discussed carries maturity tag (b), being experimental or pilot
compilation, with no statistical work-programme entry and no revision policy.

Three findings follow. Read against verbatim constituent data items rather than indicator names, the
three ecosystem condition indicators are definitionally matched to account output rather than
category-mismatched, which corrects a judgement in earlier work, including an earlier provisional
mapping by the present authors that scored account-derivable shares at figures moving from 61% to 39%
to 2.8% as the assumed indicator set changed, the last over a 36-indicator denominator the register
does not have (Section 5.2). The account types Indonesia has compiled at pilot scale bear on a small
share of the composite's published weight, whilst the types bearing on a share several times larger,
being social classification and governance, are the least developed anywhere and are compiled in
Indonesia at no maturity above methodology under development (Section 7.3). Neither share describes
achievable score improvement, as both describe the share of the index whose production these account
types bear on, and what the mature account types offer at those indicators is a production process
rather than a stock of substitutable values (Section 9.1).


# 2. Why provincial compilation is hard

A recurring statutory obligation to score every province annually sits on top of a compilation
activity that conducts no primary collection of its own. The obligation is assigned rather than
volunteered: Perpres 12/2025 carries the IBEI as an official national development indicator, and
joint circular SEB Mendagri/Bappenas No. 2/2024 distributes a 2045 target to each province for
carriage in its long-term development plan, with Papua Pegunungan carried at no baseline, achievement
or target (Juknis, Tabel 2, p. 69 [pdf 79]). That the compilation beneath it collects nothing
directly is a design decision rather than a lapse, it is recorded in Indonesia's own statistical
metadata system, and its consequences are legible as specific strains in the published output rather
than as commentary. This section establishes both from the IBEI's own documentation, and closes by
declaring the pillar structure the rest of this article computes on, since the handbook prints two.

## 2.1 Compilation without collection

SIRUSA registers the IBEI compilation as *Kompilasi Produk Administrasi* under *Statistik Sektoral*,
implemented by BAPPENAS's Directorate of Marine Affairs and Fisheries, with an empty *Identitas
Rekomendasi* (kegiatan 94936, retrieved 2026-08-13). The activity design is secondary compilation
throughout. Collection mode is *Pengumpulan data sekunder*, the instrument is *Lainnya: Publikasi KKP
dan BPS*, the unit is the province, and the register records no pilot survey, no non-response
adjustment, no staff training, zero supervisors and zero enumerators. The index's data supply is
therefore fixed to whatever KKP and BPS have already published, at the resolution and vintage they
published it. The collection capability the index draws on is real and sits with those two producers,
which is the statutory division this article's framing holds to: BAPPENAS plans, whilst MMAF and BPS
produce statistics. What the register establishes is not an absence of capability in the system but
the absence of any collection instrument inside the compilation itself, so the index can acquire
nothing that its two suppliers have not already published.

The window is narrow and is published from two directions. SIRUSA schedules collection from 1 April to
1 May 2025 and dissemination from 1 August to 1 September 2025, whilst the Juknis puts the first phase
of its monitoring and evaluation cycle, covering the circular, the timeline, the standard reporting
format and contact points, in October to December of year *n* (p. 71 [pdf 81]). Read together these
imply that an upstream figure not public by roughly April of year *n+1* cannot enter that cycle's
index. They are stated conditionally, as neither document reconciles them and only the first phase was
extracted into the corpus `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. Reference periods compound the problem in two independent
ways. Variables 12 to 14 carry T-2 where the register otherwise carries T-1, so a two-year lag sits
inside a single index vintage. Separately, the same reference periods appear twice in the source with
different renderings, as T-1 in the numbered variable table and as 2024 in the summary table, so a
prospective supplier cannot infer which vintage will be used.

## 2.2 What the published output records

Two defects carry the argument, and both are failures of provenance rather than of arithmetic.

Gaps are filled by prediction, and the prediction is not recorded. Annex 1 of the Report specifies a
machine-learning framework predicting missing values from socio-economic proxies under two scenarios,
with SUSENAS named as the proxy source (pp. 64–66 [pdf 76–78]). The framework itself is
specified in detail, and what is missing is downstream of it: the annex names no imputed indicator, no count of imputed
cells, no selected model, no achieved error, and no proxy beyond SUSENAS. Under the first scenario an
indicator absent in every province is predicted for every province. No reader of any published table
can therefore distinguish a measured provincial value from a predicted one, and which indicators were
imputed is unrecoverable from the published record, the annex naming none of them `[EVIDENCE GAP]`.
It must not be inferred from which indicators look hard to collect.

Three provinces record no value across three sub-pillars. Papua Barat Daya, Papua Selatan and Papua
Tengah, three of the four provinces created in Papua in 2022, each record 0.00 for marine-based
industry, technology and governance, and Papua Tengah's headline value of 8.51 is the lowest in the
annex (Report p. 70 [pdf 82]). Juknis Tabel 2, reproducing the SEB values, carries the same three
provinces at 2023 baselines of 49.25, 33.50 and 21.48 against 2025 achievements of 13.16, 12.25 and
8.51 (p. 69 [pdf 79]), the two documents heading those columns differently. Whether the zeros reflect
data unavailability or a measured zero is not stated in either document, so they are reported as
printed and read as consistent with a compilation discontinuity following the 2022 provincial split
rather than as proof of one `[ASSUMPTION]`. This is where a new administrative unit needs a spatially
re-aggregable source rather than a republished provincial total (Section 3).

Three further symptoms are recorded in Table 1 rather than argued here, being an underived rescaling
factor printed beneath all four regional tables, sub-pillar values far above the nominal scale on
tables headed *Normalized*, and provincial 2045 targets above the national target. Each is
uninterrogable from the published record, and none can be resolved without documents this study could
not obtain.

The denominator of the obligation is itself unstable inside the guidance that creates it. Indonesia
has 38 provinces, whilst the Report's Annex 2 tabulates 37, Tabel 2 of the Juknis lists 38 rows plus a
national row with Papua Pegunungan carrying a dash in all three columns, and the monitoring chapter
asserts comparability over *37 provinsi* (p. 71 [pdf 81]). The number used in any sentence here is the
number that sentence is about.

## 2.3 Inconsistencies and pillar locus

The handbook prints two pillar structures, four in Tabel 1 with an *Enabler (teknologi & tata kelola)*
pillar holding indicators 15 to 18 and three in Tabel 4, Tabel 5 and Gambar 11, and this article does
not resolve it: all weight arithmetic here follows the three-pillar structure, for the stated reason
that weights are published only there, whilst register rows keep their Tabel 1 labels. The choice is
material to indicators 15 to 18, of which only indicator 18 bears on any figure computed here
(Section 7.3), and immaterial to every indicator in the Tier A mapping, which sits in the environment
pillar under both loci `[ASSUMPTION]`. The declaration is therefore immaterial to the Tier A
arithmetic of Section 5 and material to Section 7, where the economy pillar's account-relevant share
of about 26.1% and the governance share of about 5.15% are computed over precisely the pillars across
which indicators 15 to 18 move, so both are structure-dependent figures for which no four-pillar
equivalent can be given from the published weights. A second contradiction bears on how any published value should
be read, as one printed page asserts both a rescaling into a 1 to 100 range and an unbounded principal
component score that may be negative (Report p. 33 [pdf 45]), which is what makes a technology
sub-pillar of 483.43 a traceability failure rather than a large number.

What the section describes is a property of the compilation design rather than of the institutions
operating it. The symptoms in Table 1 are what an inherited coverage, resolution and vintage look like
in print, and each is therefore addressable upstream, in what the compilation is able to draw on,
rather than in how BAPPENAS compiles from the supply it currently has.

# 3. What makes accounts infrastructure

Compilation infrastructure, as the term is used here, means a production system that turns primary
observation into a statistical output on a repeating cycle, carrying its own spatial unit, provenance
chain and revision rule. What makes ocean accounts compilation infrastructure is therefore not the
data they contain but four properties of the production process: a standing unit and classification
definition, fixed by the account types themselves, a balancing identity that renders an unexplained
change a visible residual, a spatial basis re-aggregable to any reporting boundary from one
compilation, and documented vintage and revision. The four-property scheme is this study's synthesis
rather than a construct the standard names as such, assembled from provisions the standard does state
separately, being the account structures and their balancing identity (United Nations et al., 2024,
paras 4.14–4.17, pp. 86–88), the treatment of basic spatial units and accounting areas (App. A1.1,
para. A1.3), and the consistency and revision requirements (para. 1.60, p. 18). Each is a property of
accounting rather than of any particular dataset. Those account types also divide against the register along a line that organises
the rest of this article (Table 2), as extent and condition produce quantities the 44 indicators
already demand whilst ecosystem services and monetary asset accounts produce quantities appearing
nowhere among them.

The balancing identity is where the production argument lives. Closing stock equals opening stock plus
additions less reductions, which makes an unexplained movement fail to balance rather than pass
unnoticed. The extent account adds a further discipline, separating additions into managed and
unmanaged expansion and reductions into managed and unmanaged reduction, so that the nature of a
change and the policy response available to it are recorded rather than inferred (United Nations
et al., 2024, paras 4.14–4.15, p. 86), and so that an addition reported without its concurrent
reduction states gross planting where a reader would infer net gain. The contrast with the index is one of production
rather than arithmetic, as its standardisation and principal-component weighting are specified in
detail and what is absent is any step at which an inconsistency would be forced to surface (Section
2). The measured quantity differs too. An account reports a stock in physical units against a stated
reference, whilst SIRUSA records that a lower index value, including a negative one, indicates
performance lagging relative to other provinces (SIRUSA, indikator 105371, Interpretasi, retrieved
2026-08-13). A hectare figure is comparable across years without recomputing every other province, and
a weighted standardised score is not.

The spatial property needs stating more carefully than it usually is. Basic spatial units retain their
place in SEEA Ecosystem Accounting but are "regarded as a means of implementing the ecosystem
accounting approach rather than as elements in a nested hierarchy" (SEEA EA 2021, App. A1.1, para.
A1.3), the ecosystem asset being the statistical unit and the reporting geography a compiler-delineated
accounting area. Provincial and district figures are accordingly two aggregations of one production
run rather than two data requests, but only where the grid, the minimum mapping unit, the landward and
seaward boundaries and the vintage are documented and held constant. That is a conditional property of
a well-specified account rather than an inherent property of accounts, and Rahayu et al. (2024)
measure what getting it wrong costs (Section 5.2). Whether cell boundaries nest cleanly within
provincial, fisheries-management and marine-spatial-planning frames is asserted rather than evidenced
`[ASSUMPTION]`, and no adopted national grid standard has been located `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. Both are
conditions precedent (Section 6).

Provenance is the property that speaks to traceability. An account is compiled to be consistent over
time and with respect to concepts and classifications (United Nations et al., 2024, para. 1.60,
p. 18), which requires the mapping
inputs, their vintage and their spatial resolution to be documented and held constant, so that patchy
coverage becomes reportable rather than concealable and a measured value stays distinguishable from a
modelled one after aggregation, where the index publishes no microdata and no per-indicator imputation
record (Section 2). Revision is likewise a property of the account form, the standard requiring
previous estimates to be revised where updated imagery, methods or classifications permit a
reassessment, with no distinct revision entry recorded and the size of the revision available as the
difference between the historical and the revised account for the same period (para. 4.17, p. 88).

A reviewer may object that a well-documented statistical register could supply all four properties
without any account being compiled, and on two of the four the objection holds outright. A standing
unit and classification definition is an act of codification, requiring a published definition of the
mapping unit, the ecosystem classes and the landward and seaward limits, and nothing about compiling
an account is needed to write one down. Documented vintage and provenance is likewise a metadata
requirement that any well-run statistical register discharges, and the spatial property is
intermediate, since a register can mandate a grid and a minimum mapping unit but cannot by itself
produce the re-aggregable cell-level tabulation the mandate presupposes. What does not reduce to
codification is the residual: the balancing identity, which forces an unexplained movement to appear
as a failure to balance rather than as a revised level, and the revision rule that alters the opening
and closing entries themselves rather than adding a separate revision line, so that the size of a
revision is recoverable by difference. Those two are properties of a compiled account because they are
properties of double-entry over a stock, not statements about a stock. The residual is deliberately
narrow, and the article's claim is correspondingly narrow, that the near-term contribution of accounts
at the indicators they touch is that residual and the production cycle carrying it, rather than a
volume of otherwise unavailable values (Section 9.1). Nothing here asserts an Indonesian capability.

# 4. What Indonesia has already compiled

Indonesian ocean accounting has produced a compiled record small enough to inventory item by item,
carrying a maturity tag on every claim, where the tag describes the datum rather than the document
reporting it. Two findings follow, and they pull against each other. That accounting has produced the
measurement basis IBEI indicators 19, 20 and 21 are defined over, being ecosystem area on a spatial
grid with cover measured against documented reference levels, whilst it has not produced the form the
index specifies, which is the share of area falling in a cover class rather than a mean cover value.
It has produced that basis once, in one marine protected area, in one province, and the properties an
annually reported official index requires of a supplier are not yet evidenced in the obtained record.

## 4.1 The compiled record

The compiled record comprises one pilot in a marine protected area, being Gili Matra Marine
Recreational Park off north Lombok, together with one peer-reviewed grid-optimisation study at Saleh
Bay, Sumbawa (Rahayu et al., 2024). Both sit in West Nusa Tenggara, so the single province carrying a
documented published compilation located by this study is NTB. A national Ocean Accounts Dashboard was
launched in July 2025 and is described by GOAP as publicly available and interactive on a harmonised
spatial frame (GOAP country page, retrieved 2026-08-13), a programme self-statement about a platform
rather than a statistical release, and one that evidences no compilation beyond the pilot record
below.

The pilot's footprint belongs in its own units, as the smallness is part of the finding: 2,980.84 ha
on a 25 m by 25 m marine basic spatial unit (MBSU) grid of 48,211 cells, opening in 2015 and closing
in 2021, those years chosen on data availability (MMAF, 2022, pp. 30, 39 [pdf 44, 53]; Wulandari and
Jannah, 2023, slide 11). The two figures do not reconcile arithmetically, as 48,211 cells at 625 m2
each is 3,013.19 ha against the 2,980.84 ha printed, a shortfall of 32.35 ha or 1.1% that would be
consistent with cells clipped at the park boundary but is nowhere explained in the report, and is
recorded here as observed rather than resolved `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. Against a statutory obligation to score every province annually (Section 2),
that is an area smaller than many single districts, compiled once.

Maturity is the programme's own self-description rather than an external verdict. GOAP labels the
three 2022 MMAF outputs 'preliminary consultation drafts', and BPS lists scaling up to the total
Indonesian marine area among its outstanding challenges (Tasriah et al., 2022, slide 9). Every
Indonesian ocean account output discussed here therefore carries maturity tag (b), experimental or
pilot compilation. There is no BPS ocean account release, no entry in the national statistical work
programme, no published revision policy, and no published compilation cost per site or per unit, which
constrains what may responsibly be recommended (Sections 6 and 10). The ecosystem scope is confined to
mangrove, seagrass and coral reef.

## 4.2 One maturity, three completenesses

The claim that Indonesia has ocean accounts decomposes into three claims of unequal strength. All
three account types carry the same maturity tag (b), and what separates them is completeness of the
account form rather than the standing of the compilation producing it.

Extent is the only account type with a verified opening, change and closing series. The pilot
publishes a complete asset account (MMAF, 2022, tab. 5, p. 52 [pdf 66]) in which coral falls from
259.50 ha to 247.50 ha, seagrass rises from 76.75 ha to 102.50 ha and mangrove falls from 21.50 ha to
10.69 ha, with additions and reductions itemised as natural expansion, natural regression and managed
regression, and with an ecosystem-conversion matrix at tab. 6 (p. 53 [pdf 67]). This is the stock and
flow identity in Indonesian practice rather than asserted from the SEEA Ecosystem Accounting standard.

Extent is also where two official series diverge. A BPS presentation reports the same pilot,
ecosystems and years throughout with coral falling from 183.34 ha to 165.08 ha, seagrass rising from
56.50 ha to 64.93 ha and mangrove falling from 13.53 ha to 6.15 ha (Tasriah et al., 2022, slide 3).
Both series trace to one layer, being the Geospatial Information Agency's 2020 mapping validated by
2021 field survey, which MMAF overlaid on the MBSU grid with 194 verification points (pp. 23, 30 [pdf
37, 44]) and which BPS cites as BIG (2020), validated 2021. Neither document states its relationship
to the other and no reconciliation is published, so the discrepancy stands as an open conflict between
two official documents, neither averaged nor adjudicated here `[EVIDENCE GAP]`.

The divergence is not a constant offset, which is the property that matters for an account. Taking the
ratio of the BPS figure to the MMAF figure ecosystem by ecosystem and year by year gives coral 0.707
in 2015 against 0.667 in 2021, seagrass 0.736 against 0.633 and mangrove 0.629 against 0.575, so the
ratio moves both across ecosystems and across the accounting window and cannot be explained as a
single boundary clip, a units convention or a fixed classification threshold. The consequence is that
the two series disagree on the change and not merely on the level, MMAF recording a coral loss of
12.00 ha over the window against a BPS loss of 18.26 ha. Change is the quantity a balancing identity
exists to report, so the one compiled Indonesian account has produced two closing stocks and two
deltas for the same site, and that fact is carried into the limitations and interrogated against this
article's own provenance claim (Sections 8.5 and 9.1).

Condition is compiled in a limited form, being cover-based measurement at Tier 1 to Tier 2 read
against documented reference bands rather than a normalised multi-indicator composite. Coral was
measured by underwater photo transect with CPCe analysis following Muttaqin et al. (2020), seagrass by
the Seagrass Watch protocol and mangrove by plot sampling (MMAF, 2022, pp. 31–33 [pdf 45–47]). Values
are classified against published reference levels, with 16.99% live hard coral cover in 2021 read
against the bands of Giyanto et al. (2017), and mangrove classified as good at a density of at least
1,500 individuals per hectare under Ministry of Environment Decree 201/2004 (pp. 40, 47 [pdf 54, 61]).
That the pilot measures cover as a value against a documented reference level, rather than scoring it
as a composite, is both a limit of the account and what leaves the register's class-tabulation form
within reach (Section 5.2).

Two facts are recorded here without adjudication. The pilot reports mean cover by zone rather than
share of area by class, so producing indicators 19, 20 and 21 from it would require re-tabulating the
underlying transect and grid data to a different class boundary `[ASSUMPTION]`. And 16.99% cover is
reported, then classified as moderate against a band running from 19% to 35%, a tension recorded as
observed and unexplained.

Valuation is the least developed of the three and may not be described as an ecosystem services
account in physical units. What exists is
benefit-transferred monetary valuation of ecosystem functions at pilot scale rather than supply and
use of ecosystem services in physical units. Coral values are transferred from Bohol (Samonte-Tan et
al., 2007) and the Mariana Islands (van Beukering et al., 2006), seagrass from Mexico, and mangrove
from Gazi Bay, Kenya (Hoberg, 2011) and Bintuni Bay, Indonesia (Ruitenbeek, 1992), giving pilot totals
rising from IDR 45.24 bn per year in 2015 to IDR 64.17 bn per year in 2021 (pp. 36, 60 [pdf 50, 74]).
Benefit transfer is not the exchange-value basis the SEEA Ecosystem Accounting standard requires, so
no exchange-value or depreciation claim here can rest on it, a limit BPS states itself in listing the
absence of implemented standard valuation methods among its challenges (Tasriah et al., 2022, slide
9). On this evidence, no ecosystem services account in physical units has been compiled in Indonesia.

## 4.3 Institutional and spatial standing

A national index would require of an account supplier a standing custodian carrying a periodicity and
a revision policy, together with an adopted spatial standard. Both are unsettled on the primary
record, and both on the programme's own instruments rather than by inference from silence.

Kepmen KP 44/2023, which constituted the Tim Penyusun Neraca Sumber Daya Laut on 16 February 2023,
cuts both ways. It establishes five working groups and names five accounts, covering ecosystem extent
and condition with monetary value, flows to the economy, waste flows, the ocean economy and
governance, and it places the BPS Director of Production Accounts in the chair of environmental
accounts implementation (Pokja C), with BAPPENAS a single member of the governance working group
(Pokja D) rather than a co-lead. Its own diktum KEEMPAT set the team's term to expire on 31 December
2023, and no renewal instrument was located. The July 2025 dashboard launch indicates continued
activity under some instrument not obtained, so the defensible statement is that no renewal instrument
was located rather than that the mandate lapsed `[EVIDENCE GAP]`.

Rahayu et al. (2024), the only peer-reviewed Indonesian account source, state that a standardised grid
has yet to be established. Grid choice changes the extent tabulation itself, which makes an adopted
grid a substantive prerequisite for supply rather than a technical detail (Section 6).

One bounded search result is reported on what this study read rather than on an exhaustive survey. The
searched set was assembled between 11 and 16 August 2026 and comprises the MMAF 2022 series, the BPS
materials prepared for the SEEA workshops, Rahayu et al. (2024), Kepmen KP 44/2023, the GOAP country
page (retrieved 2026-08-13) and the SIRUSA metadata for the IBEI compilation activity (kegiatan 94936
and indikator 105371, both retrieved 2026-08-13), searched bilingually on the Indonesian terms *neraca
laut*, *akun laut*, *neraca ekosistem*, *unit spasial dasar laut*, *peta jalan ekonomi biru* and *Satu
Data Indonesia* together with their English equivalents, with no date restriction applied. Within that
set, no Indonesian statistical register supplying provenance, spatial unit, vintage and revision
policy for marine ecosystem extent and condition products, separately from an account structure, was
encountered `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. No dedicated search of the BPS statistical catalogue or of the Satu Data
Indonesia portal was undertaken, and no engagement with any Indonesian agency was sought, so the
statement is bounded to the named set and is not a claim about the Indonesian statistical system.

Three properties remain unestablished by this inventory: national coverage, an annual cycle capable of
meeting the index's T-1 reporting window, and a documented custodial chain. What the record supports
is narrower than a claim that supply exists, being a method demonstrated in one province at pilot
scale, on one vintage, with cover-based condition and benefit-transferred money.

# 5. Mapping: the compiled accounts

Of the register's 44 rows, five are the points at which an ocean account touches the IBEI at all.
Three of them, indicators 19, 20 and 21, name quantities that an account's output structure already
contains, whilst indicators 24 and 27 sit adjacent to account output and are disposed of here rather
than claimed. Which rows qualify is established by a membership test applied to the verbatim
constituent data items of the register, not to indicator names, indicator names having proved an
unreliable guide in earlier assessments.

## 5.1 What Tier A means

The unit of analysis is the register row, of which there are 44, and the object tested is not the row's
title but the *Data Pembentuk Indikator* printed against it, the constituent data items that specify
what must be measured. Across the 44 rows those items decompose into 62 distinct constituent data
items, some rows naming one and others several (Juknis, Tabel 1, pp. 11–13 [pdf 21–23], this study's
count over the register as printed). The test is applied to all 62 in Indonesian, and grades each row
on one of three grounds. A row is *matched* where a constituent data item names a quantity that a
defined SEEA Ecosystem Accounting account output contains, which yields indicators 19, 20 and 21,
n = 3. A row is *partial* where account output supplies a component of the quantity but not the
quantity itself, which yields indicator 24, n = 1. A row is a *provenance question* where account
output bears on the row without supplying it, which yields indicator 27, n = 1. Tier A is the union of
the three grades and has five members out of 44. Every other row is scored none, on the ground that no
constituent data item in it names a quantity any account output contains.

Membership is therefore a claim about correspondence between two published specifications, being the
index's register and the SEEA Ecosystem Accounting output structure, and it carries no claim about
compilation state. The distinction matters because a label such as 'can serve now' collapses the two
axes, asserting a production capability from a definitional property.

On the second axis the concession is complete. Every Indonesian ocean account output carries maturity
tag (b) (Section 4.1), and none has published any of the five indicator values, for any province or
any site `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. Every capability claim here is accordingly a claim about definitional
correspondence and a demonstrated input layer, never about an existing output. The tier is named on
the first axis and conceded on the second at each use: Tier A, definitionally matched, compilation
demonstrated at pilot scale. All five rows sit in the environment pillar under both pillar loci, so
the unresolved conflict does not reach this section's arithmetic.

What the test yields is a correction of the literature rather than a capability claim. Earlier
assessments judged that Indonesia lacks the condition account indicators 19, 20 and 21 require. Those
indicators do not require one, so the judgement answers a question the index does not pose, and the
barrier at these rows is compilation and geographic coverage rather than measurement target.

## 5.2 The cover-class match, and its frays

The three condition indicators share one grammatical form. Indicator 19 asks for the *Persentase
kawasan terumbu karang dengan tutupan karang hidup antara 50-100% per provinsi*, indicator 20 for the
*Persentase kawasan lamun dengan tutupan lamun hidup antara 50-100% per provinsi*, and indicator 21
for the *Persentase kawasan mangrove dengan kerapatan tutupan antara 50-100% per provinsi* (Juknis,
Tabel 1, p. 12 [pdf 22]). Each names a percentage of area falling within a stated cover or density
band, per province, which is a tabulation of extent by class rather than a normalised index against a
reference condition.

What the pilot sets beside that specification is the input layer and not the tabulation. The MBSU
extent surface exists (Section 4.1), and cover is measured at survey stations, giving live coral cover
of 18.11% in 2016 and live hard coral cover of 16.99% in 2021, which the report does not state to be
the same measure and which are therefore not read here as a series, and seagrass cover averaging
32.28% with a station maximum of 82.01% (MMAF, 2022, pp. 40, 45 [pdf 54, 59]). The 2021 coral figure is
unreconciled in the source, where the narrative reports 16.99% whilst the report's own tab. 3 on the same page prints
an average of 18.36%. The cross-tabulation of extent by cover class is not published, and the step
from the input layer to indicator 19 is one tabulation that has not been performed.

The match frays at four edges, and the first two are definitional. The 50% threshold is the index's
own and matches no Indonesian condition class boundary in use, as the pilot classifies reef condition
on the bands of Giyanto et al. (2017), where moderate runs from 19% to below 35% live hard coral.
Reclassifying a continuous per-cell cover surface at 50% would be arithmetically trivial, but that
holds only where condition is mapped wall to wall per grid cell rather than surveyed at stations, and
no obtained document evidences a wall-to-wall condition surface `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. Where national
condition data exist only as classes pre-binned on the Giyanto boundaries, the 50% cut cannot be
recovered, and the coral row becomes a definition gap rather than a coverage gap `[ASSUMPTION]`. The
mangrove unit differs outright: indicator 21 asks for canopy density as a percentage, whilst the pilot
reports mangrove canopy cover in m² per ha and stem density in individuals per ha, with no documented
conversion between them (p. 47 [pdf 61]) `[EVIDENCE GAP]`.

The second two edges are unfixed choices. Neither the minimum mapping unit nor the denominator is
fixed by the register or by the SIRUSA definitions, so whether the percentage is taken over mapped
ecosystem extent or over total provincial marine area remains a definitional residue `[ASSUMPTION]`.
And the extent estimate is itself a function of the grid rule, as Rahayu et al. (2024) report mangrove
error of 44.06% under a 25 m hierarchy scheme against 1.59% under 25 m dominance. Until a grid rule is
adopted, the account's figure is a family of figures, and the index would be importing a
methodological choice.

A vintage gap sits alongside the definitional ones, as the only verified account series closes in 2021
against the index's T-1 requirement and its unsettled rendering (Section 2.1).

Restated after those concessions, the correction narrows and holds. The measurement task reduces from
constructing reference-condition norms for three ecosystems in every province to classifying cover per
cell against a single threshold, which is a smaller object to build, and the wall-to-wall condition
surface it needs remains unbuilt. Where these three values are currently predicted under the
imputation framework the Report publishes (pp. 64–66 [pdf 76–78]), supplying them from an account
would replace a prediction with a measurement, though which indicators were imputed, for which
provinces, is not recoverable `[EVIDENCE GAP]`.

## 5.3 An addition term and a designation

Indicator 24 asks for the *Total luas kawasan mangrove, rawa, dan lahan gambut yang direstorasi per
provinsi*, glossed in SIRUSA as degraded area subsequently replanted, which is an activity input
measured in hectares planted. The pilot's asset account carries a managed-expansion line in its
structure that is unpopulated at Gili Matra, where every addition is natural expansion and the only
managed entry is 11.19 ha of managed mangrove regression (MMAF, 2022, tab. 5, p. 52 [pdf 66]). The
correspondence at this row is therefore structural rather than demonstrated: an account of this form
would record realised managed change, whilst the indicator counts hectares planted. The scope
mismatch compounds it, as the indicator's three land covers are mangrove, swamp and peatland against
the marine account's mangrove, seagrass and coral reef, so even complete national coverage would leave
indicator 24 partly unserved.

The pilot's seagrass row illustrates what the distinction buys. Of the 51.50 ha gained, 8.25 ha came
from former reef and 43.25 ha from other substrate, against 25.75 ha lost to coral and to other
substrate. A hectares-planted count cannot see either side of that ledger, whilst the account's change
matrix records which is which.

Indicator 27 asks for the *Total luas kawasan konservasi perairan yang ditetapkan oleh KKP, KLHK, dan
Pemerintah Daerah per provinsi*, and is in the tier for a different reason again. At 22.0% it is the
largest single variable in the resource-quality sub-pillar, about 4.06% of the composite, and it needs
no account to be produced: designated area is an administrative datum already held by its custodians
and broken out in SIRUSA as nationally managed, regionally managed and KLHK-managed areas. The
account's near-term contribution here is reconciliation, being the attachment of mapped ecosystem
extent to the designated polygon as the pilot does by zone, and not supply.

## 5.4 How the gaps sort

Sorted by the kind of gap that remains, the tier splits three ways (Table 3). Indicators 19 and 20 are
coverage and vintage rows on sound definitions, implying a compilation programme beyond the pilot
sites and inside the index's reporting window (Section 6). Indicators 21 and 24 are
definition rows no amount of coverage will close, the first on units and the second on the difference
between an activity and an outcome, implying threshold, unit and denominator rules agreed with the
index's custodian. Indicator 27 is not a gap but a provenance question. A fourth gap kind, the spatial
standard, is cross-cutting rather than attaching to any row, and implies an adopted national grid and
clipping rule. That sort, and not the tier's total weight, is what tells a compiler what to do next.

The weight arithmetic is shown openly as a multiplication, carried to two decimal places from the
one-decimal percentages the handbook prints and reported in prose to one decimal with 'about', a
convention held throughout the article. The environment pillar carries 36.9% of the composite and the
resource-quality sub-pillar 50.0% of the pillar (Juknis, Tabel 4 and Tabel 5, p. 94 [pdf 104]), giving
18.45% of the composite, of which the five Tier A variables sum to about 7.64% (Table 3). That total
is tagged `[CONTESTED DENOMINATOR]` throughout this article, and the tag names one specific objection
rather than the pillar conflict, which does not reach this arithmetic: 7.64% is a defensible measure of
the share of the index these account types bear on, and an indefensible measure of the share an
account would supply, because most of it sits at rows the mapping has itself disqualified from supply.
The decomposition is therefore reported in three parts, as the tier's own sort requires: about 2.33%
at the three matched condition rows, about 1.25% at indicator 24 as a partial, and about 4.06% at
indicator 27, which the account does not supply. The share at which an account is the prospective
supply route is accordingly about 2.33%, rising to about 3.58% only if indicator 24's structural
correspondence is credited in full, which the activity-against-outcome mismatch and the swamp and
peatland scope gap indicate it should not be (Section 5.3). The lower figure is the operative one and
the upper an outer bound. Whether the printed percentages are raw first principal component loadings
or normalised contributions is not stated, so these products are indicative rather than exact
`[ASSUMPTION]`, and the Tinggi, Sedang and Rendah bands are the handbook's own rank-based
simplification. None of these figures travels alone, as the account types bearing on the larger shares
of the index, being social classification and governance, are the least developed anywhere, and are
priced in Section 7.3.

Under the published weights that share is small, so substituting account-derived values would be
unlikely to move the composite materially, though weights derived by principal components are
re-estimated from the data, so a substitution is not weight-invariant.

Neither reading is the finding. What changes at these rows is how the numbers are made. A cover-class
percentage compiled from a gridded extent surface and a classified cover layer carries its own spatial
unit, its own opening and closing stocks, and a provenance chain back to the imagery and field survey
behind it, and it is reproducible in the next cycle by repeating the compilation. A value taken from a
secondary publication, or predicted where no publication exists, carries none of those properties, and
the published record does not distinguish the two (Section 2). That is a production-process claim
about five rows, not a claim about the index's score. Nothing here proposes that accounts be compiled
in order to serve the index, and nothing moves an indicator out of its custodian's hands, on which the
register is silent in a way worth recording: no producing agency is stated for any of the 44 rows
`[EVIDENCE GAP]`.

# 6. Conditions precedent for supply

A definitional match is not a supply arrangement. Five conditions must hold before pilot account output
could serve the IBEI as a repeating input rather than a single demonstration, none is satisfied in the
obtained record, and they are not equally binding (Table 4). Three are one-off acts of codification
that a single decision by MMAF, BPS or BAPPENAS could discharge. Two are discharged only by sustaining
a production cycle, and those two are what decide whether the mapping in Section 5 is a recommendation
or a wish. The register would be refuted by producing an adopted national marine spatial standard, a
BPS statistical work-programme entry for a marine ecosystem account, or an extent compilation covering
more than the pilot sites and closing after 2021. Each condition is read against the accounting
standard where the standard speaks to it, and is otherwise this study's own specification, which is
stated as such at each row.

## 6.1 The test: a dated window

The obligation is recurring and annual, set by Perpres 12/2025 and SEB 2/2024 and registered at annual
frequency in SIRUSA, and the monitoring architecture fixes its first phase at October to December of
year *n* (Section 2.1). It can receive only what an upstream producer has already released, at that
producer's resolution and vintage. The operative test for a prospective supplier is therefore repeatability into a dated window rather than
demonstrated capability. Two of the system's own principles are supply-side conditions in disguise,
being temporal continuity and a standardisation guaranteeing that provinces aggregate on comparable
terms, and a standardised reporting format cannot in any case be issued without stating which of the
handbook's two pillar structures it follows (Section 2.3).

## 6.2 Spatial conditions

Provincial account output is not comparable across provinces or across vintages until one minimum
mapping unit and one basic spatial unit are adopted nationally. Both spatial conditions are acts of
codification, discharged once and requiring no new compilation. The standard requires an account to be
consistent over time and with respect to concepts and classifications (United Nations et al., 2024,
para. 1.60), and requires previous estimates to be revised for continuity of the time series wherever
updated data, methods or classifications permit a reassessment of area (para. 4.17). Grid-based
spatial units are the mechanism by which an account aggregates to any administrative boundary
(Section 3), conditional on the grid existing and being national. No adopted national marine grid or minimum mapping unit was found in any
obtained Indonesian document, having searched the set stated at Table 4 `[EVIDENCE GAP]`, whilst the
pilot literature records different
resolutions at different sites, the case the rule exists to prevent.

The second spatial condition is a codified clipping rule. Provincial marine authority extends to 12
nautical miles under Law 23/2014, cited here at second hand and not read at source `[EVIDENCE GAP]`,
so an account reaching further must be clipped before it can yield a provincial number. The standard
leaves the accounting area to the compiler (para. 2.12) and supplies no sub-national allocation rule
for an asset spanning a boundary, so the convention this study takes as necessary, being allocation by
physical extent within each side with condition compiled per spatial unit rather than inherited whole,
is a specification rather than a citation `[ASSUMPTION]`, and neither it nor the statutory line is
codified in any account obtained by this study `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. One consequence follows from the
standard directly. Condition is not additive across areas, its variable account being an area-weighted
arithmetic mean over the assets of an ecosystem type (para. 5.54, p. 105), so because indicators 19, 20
and 21 ask for a ratio, a provincial value cannot be recovered by averaging district values, and a
supplier would need to publish the denominator alongside the percentage for a provincial value to be
recoverable.
Clean nesting of cell boundaries across administrative, fisheries-management and marine-spatial-planning
frames is asserted rather than evidenced `[ASSUMPTION]`, and forms part of this condition rather than a
property that may be assumed.

## 6.3 Temporal conditions

The reference period is the binding constraint on the mapped indicators, and it binds on repetition
rather than on speed. SIRUSA specifies T-1 for all five, being variables 19, 20, 21, 24 and 27, the
same five matched definitionally in Section 5. The single verified Indonesian compilation closes at
2021 and was published the following year, a lag the T-1 requirement can accommodate. What
is absent is the second vintage. One compilation, however timely, cannot meet a T-1 requirement in any subsequent
cycle, and no repeat compilation of the pilot sites was found in any obtained document
`[EVIDENCE GAP]`. This condition is therefore recurring rather than one-off, discharged by running the
cycle again and not by a decision, and it survives the index's own inconsistent rendering of its
reference periods (Section 2.1) `[ASSUMPTION]`. Sustaining a cycle is a resourcing question, and no
compilation cost was located in the public record, so this study cannot price any condition in the
register.

A revision policy is the second recurring condition. The standard already supplies its substance,
requiring revision of previous estimates for continuity of the series, recording no distinct revision
entry but altering the opening, closing, addition and reduction entries themselves, and allowing the
size of a revision to be shown as the difference between the historical and the revised account for
the same period (paras 4.17–4.18, p. 88). What is unwritten is the Indonesian instrument that would
designate a baseline year and disclose where the latest extent data refer to an earlier period, and
the index's own principle of controlled adaptability presupposes that discipline on the supply side.
No revision policy was found for any Indonesian ocean account output in the searched set
`[EVIDENCE GAP]`. The consequence is specific: an account silently improved between vintages would
move a province's index value with no ecosystem change behind it, and nothing in the published record
would flag that. The instrument requires no new compilation, being a statement of when a series is
restated and how a provisional estimate is marked.

## 6.4 The institutional condition

Account output cannot become a durable input to an official index whilst it remains an unregistered
ministry product feeding an activity itself registered only as sectoral statistics. The IBEI is
recorded in SIRUSA as *Kompilasi Produk Administrasi* under *Statistik Sektoral* with an empty
*Identitas Rekomendasi* (kegiatan 94936), whilst the Juknis names BAPPENAS as *pengampu* of the
calculation (p. 68 [pdf 78]). Indonesian ocean account output is an MMAF product constituted under
Kepmen KP 44/2023 (Section 4.3), and no BPS statistical work-programme entry appears in any document
obtained by this study, no dedicated search of the BPS statistical catalogue having been undertaken
`[EVIDENCE GAP]`. The condition is unmet against an instrument whose own term has run, that Kepmen
having set the team's expiry at 31 December 2023 with no renewal instrument located (Section 4.3), so
a supply arrangement would be settled by whichever body now holds the coordinating function rather
than by the standing arrangement the record documents. Two sectoral products feeding one another does not by itself
constitute an accredited statistical input. The condition is a documented supply arrangement
between MMAF as producer, BPS as statistical authority and BAPPENAS as compiler, whose minimum content
is stated here as this study's own specification rather than taken from any standard, being scope,
frequency, quality standards, confidentiality, format and dispute resolution `[ASSUMPTION]`. Written
that way it is a short document rather than a programme. That
accreditation would be the route is inferred from the index's own registration rather than stated
anywhere, so it is put as something BPS and MMAF would have to settle `[ASSUMPTION]`. The gap runs in both directions, since no
microdata are released (Section 3), so even a compliant supplier could not verify that its value
survived into the published provincial score. This condition, like the two spatial ones, is discharged
once, and all three are conditions the index would need met rather than obligations the accounts
programme owes it.

## 6.5 Why coverage must lead insertion

One design recommendation follows from the conditions. Where a province has no account, an account-derived indicator would itself be predicted from
socio-economic proxies under the framework the Report publishes (pp. 64–66 [pdf 76–78]), so an
environmental value would become partly a function of economic and social covariates. The framework's
first scenario trains on the previous cycle's historical data, which a newly introduced indicator does
not have. Insertion should
therefore be phased behind coverage: compile first, insert second. This is a sequencing recommendation
and not a change to the index's method.

Proportion should temper the register. Three of the five conditions are acts of codification rather
than compilation programmes, and none of the five could be costed here (Section 6.3),
whilst the mature route they bear on is about 7.64% of the composite `[CONTESTED DENOMINATOR]`, of
which about 2.33% is the share an account would itself supply, rising to about 3.58% only if indicator
24 is credited in full (Section 5.4), against roughly 18.9% resting on account types compiled nowhere
(Section 7.3). The leverage of the mapped indicators alone
does not justify them, and the two recurring conditions decide whether even that leverage is a
prospect or a wish.

# 7. Mapping: what could be served

Beyond the five rows existing Indonesian accounts already touch, the question is not which further
indicators an account could supply but which parts of the index depend on a classification, boundary
or stock-and-flow standard that no one currently holds. The arithmetic below follows the three-pillar
locus declared earlier (Section 2.3), so every tourism, technology and governance figure carries that
locus with it. Routes are named against the accounting standard where it specifies them and against
the specified sequence of national ocean accounts (IDEEA Group, 2021) where it does not, and the
difference between those two is itself part of the finding. Every derived figure here inherits the
loadings caveat
(Section 5.4), mitigated only by each figure cross-checking against Gambar 11's independently printed
integers.

## 7.1 Tier B: reachable sub-pillars

The comparison this section builds towards is drawn between account types that would change what the
index contains, and that criterion is stated here, before any tier is priced, because it decides which
tiers enter it. Tier B fails the criterion on the content side rather than on the maturity side, as
its routes would recompile quantities the register already specifies without adding any quantity to
it, which is a different kind of contribution from the ones the inversion compares.

Four economy sub-pillars carry (15.2 + 23.6 + 19.3 + 22.1)% of the economy pillar, or 80.2% of it, at
32.6% of the composite, giving about 26.1%, which Gambar 11's integers confirm at 5 + 8 + 6 + 7 = 26
(Juknis, Tabel 5 and Gambar 11, pp. 93–94 [pdf 103–104]). The pillar's remaining weight is the
governance sub-pillar at 15.8% and the technology sub-pillar at about 4.11%, so the six sub-pillars
close the pillar to 100.11% on the printed one-decimal weights. This figure is computed under the
three-pillar structure of Tabel 4, Tabel 5 and Gambar 11, and under Tabel 1's four-pillar structure
indicators 15 to 18 leave the economy pillar for the *Enabler* pillar, whose sub-pillar shares are not
published, so no equivalent figure can be given (Section 2.3). Every constituent data item in the four
sub-pillars is an administrative quantity of the kind KKP and BPS routinely compile: production
tonnages, export tonnages, passenger and vessel counts, site counts. Whether each is published, and by
whom, cannot be established from the register, which names no producer for any of the 44 rows
`[EVIDENCE GAP]`. What is established is narrower than invariance: no route in Table 5 supplies a
quantity of a kind the register does not already specify, whilst whether recompiling those quantities
on an accounting industry boundary would change their values, and hence provincial scores, cannot be
determined here, and under weights re-estimated by principal components from the data it cannot be
assumed not to (Section 5.4). What an account adds is an industry boundary consistent with
international and Indonesian industrial classification, under which such quantities become comparable
across provinces and across cycles, this being the ocean economy account of the specified sequence
(IDEEA Group, 2021) and the case made for national accounting for the ocean by Fenichel et al.
(2020).

One correction to the intuitive fisheries mapping is worth stating in prose, as it inverts the
apparent priority. Sub-pillar A carries 15.2% of the economy pillar and therefore about 4.96% of the composite, and it is
83.0% aquaculture by published weight, being 27.3% aquaculture production, 26.3% brackish-water pond
area and 29.4% seaweed, against 6.3% for capture-fisheries volume (Tabel 6, p. 95 [pdf 105]). Reading
production against a stock assessment therefore bears on about 0.31% of the composite, being 6.3% of
that 4.96%, whilst a consistent aquaculture production boundary bears on about 4.11%, being 83.0% of
it. The intellectually interesting route is the numerically negligible one. Waste flows sit in the
same tier, where a modelled leakage estimate would become an accounted economy-to-environment flow at
about 2.75% and 0.77% of the composite, those being the marine leakage variable at 14.9% and the land
waste variable at 4.2% of the 18.45% resource-quality envelope (Tabel 6, pp. 95–96 [pdf 105–106]), and
these are reported
separately here because they sit outside the five Tier A rows and must not be folded into that total.

## 7.2 Tier C: what cannot now be measured

None of the 44 constituent data items names an ecosystem service flow, an asset value or a depletion
term. That absence is the tier's subject, and its sharpest instance is an identity rather than an
aspiration. Indicators 2 to 5 are unsigned production volumes, so a province raises its score by
producing more, and no term anywhere in the register records whether the stock producing it is being
drawn down. The missing term is depletion, defined in the SEEA Central Framework
as the using up of a stock of resources relative to rates of regeneration (United Nations et al.,
2024, para. A1.25). The obstacle is that sustainable yield by province and by stock is not published,
which makes stock assessment a prerequisite rather than an alternative.

Ecosystem services in physical units are specified by the standard in full (United Nations et al.,
2024, ch. 7) and are compiled nowhere in Indonesia (Section 4.2). The nearest candidate is coastal protection from
mangrove and reef, measured in physical units against the extent and condition layers the pilot
already produces, which is named here as the specific service rather than left as a category. Net
extent change as a stock-and-flow identity is recommended as a reported companion series and not as a
forty-fifth indicator, for a reason developed later (Section 8). Sub-pillar H is
50.0% of the environment pillar and contains only solar and hydropower variables, at 24.5% and 24.2%
for photovoltaic capacity and generation and 26.5% and 24.8% for hydropower (Tabel 6, p. 96 [pdf
106]), so half the environment pillar measures non-marine energy. An offshore energy variable is the route by
which that sub-pillar could measure something marine, which makes this a recommendation about
indicator design rather than about compilation, and its obstacle is a matter of installed Indonesian
marine capacity on which no figure was located `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. Combined presentations
(United Nations et al., 2024, paras 14.56–14.57) are the one row available immediately, being a
presentation choice rather than a data gap.

## 7.3 Social and governance accounting

Reading constituent data items rather than indicator names relocates the largest account-relevant
share of the index into the social pillar, where nothing above methodology under development has been
compiled anywhere in Indonesia. Five of the
thirteen social indicators are ratios of a fisheries-sector population to a provincial total, and the
register prints the same construction repeatedly: indicator 32 divides *Jumlah penduduk bekerja di
sektor perikanan yang menerima PKH per provinsi* by the provincial total receiving that transfer,
indicator 37 does the same for *penduduk bekerja di sektor perikanan yang memiliki jaminan kesehatan*,
and indicators 42, 43 and 44 repeat it for upper-secondary schooling, upper-secondary certification
and the Program Indonesia Pintar (Juknis, Tabel 1, p. 13 [pdf 23]). Two more presuppose the same
identification: indicator 34 is a headcount of *nelayan dan pembudidaya ikan*, and indicator 33 counts
*pekerja perempuan di sektor perikanan*, which requires that population sex-disaggregated and is
therefore a harder deliverable than the domain alone. None of the seven is computable without a
reproducible definition of the population working in the fisheries sector as a survey domain joined to
SUSENAS, SAKERNAS and poverty statistics. That is a classification deliverable, not a survey
deliverable.

The share this bears on is about 13.7% of the composite, decomposed in Table 6 so that a reader may
reject a particular row rather than the total: the PKH transfer ratio at about 4.59%, the fisher and
fisherwomen counts at about 0.97%, the three education variables carrying a domain cut at 1.91%, and
health insurance at 6.23%. The remainder of the pillar does not depend on a sector domain. Fish calorie
and protein consumption at about 14.21% is a SUSENAS consumption item, and the KKP administrative

disagrees with itself: Tabel 1 builds indicator 38 from *Jumlah penduduk miskin di setiap

held outside the 13.7% on the Tabel 1 wording, and the Tabel 6 wording would move it inside, taking
the total to about 14.0%. The components sum, to rounding, to the pillar's published 30.5%. The 13.7% is the
classification-standard claim, whilst the 30.5% is the wider claim that an ocean social account's
structure corresponds to the production of the whole pillar, and the two are not interchangeable. Which indicators require
an ocean-sector domain is this study's reading of the verbatim constituent data items and is stated in

The maturity qualification is severe and belongs in the same breath as the share. Ocean employment
and human capital appear in the specified sequence of national ocean accounts as a component to be

Governance is measured by one count. Tabel 6 gives the governance sub-pillar exactly one variable,

Indicator 27 belongs here rather than with the mapping that scored it. Its roughly 4.06% of the

# 8. What account supply does not fix

## 8.1 Extending published limitations

The index's custodian has printed its own methodological limits, and account supply reaches none of
them. Four are conceded in the index's own methods statement (Report Section 3.1.1, p. 34 [pdf 46]),
being an assumption of linear and monotonic relationships, whose worked example is that the benefit of
fishing intensity holds below a threshold whilst damage rises exponentially above it, sensitivity to
outliers, weights set on 2024 data that *mungkin saja menjadi tidak relevan untuk tahun-tahun
mendatang* should the structure or coverage of the blue economy change fundamentally, and a single
weight structure applied across a geographically and socio-economically heterogeneous archipelago. Every one is a property of the
estimator, whilst an account changes the input. The first is worth dwelling on, since the threshold
relationship BAPPENAS describes is exactly what an ecosystem account measures well, and better inputs
cannot reach a relationship the estimator represents linearly by design, which is the limitation
already printed. The remedies BAPPENAS proposes are compatible with account supply, and the first of
them, complementary sectoral analysis reported alongside the index, is compatible in turn with the
combined presentations the accounting standard describes, which summarise data from several accounts
beside related economic and social data without requiring that ecosystem services be valued in
monetary terms (United Nations et al., 2024, paras 14.56–14.57, pp. 356–357). What follows is therefore the limits register
any supplier of inputs must accept, rather than a critique of the index.

## 8.2 What aggregation discards

The index consumes a scalar. The constituent data items for indicators 19, 20 and 21 each end *per
provinsi* (Juknis, Tabel 1, p. 12 [pdf 22]), so an account compiled on a fine spatial grid discards its
within-province distribution at the moment it becomes an indicator. The loss is asymmetric in value, because the account can tell a provincial
planner which reef complex is driving a change whilst the index can report only that a percentage
moved. That loss is not a necessary property of accounts but a consequence of publishing the indicator
without the grid `[ASSUMPTION]`, so the remedy is an architecture in which both are released, which is
a publication recommendation rather than a supply one (Section 9).

The provincial boundary is an administrative line and not an ecological one (Section 6.2), and the
standard is explicit that an accounting area may be a country, a subnational administrative area, a
catchment or a protected area (United Nations et al., 2024, para. 2.12), which leaves the choice of
boundary to the compiler and its ecological consequences to the account. A reef complex straddling
that line
is divided between two accounting areas by legal artefact. Accounts make the division explicit and
auditable, which is worth having, and they do not make it ecologically meaningful, which is why the
clipping rule is a condition precedent rather than a solved problem (Section 6.2). One further limit is
inherited rather than created, since in a province without a compilation an account-derived indicator
would itself be predicted from socio-economic proxies (Section 6.5).

## 8.3 Why net extent change cannot enter

The index states its own weighting rule: an indicator with greater variability and strong correlation
to others in its domain *akan secara alami mendapatkan bobot yang lebih tinggi* (p. 29 [pdf 41]). A
signed annual difference of a large, slowly changing stock is low-variance by construction and can
change sign between cycles, so it would attract a small and unstable weight and might meet the rule
printed in the sub-pillar analysis loop as *Reversi nilai indikator (atau drop indikator) jika
ditemukan relasi negatif* (p. 30 [pdf 42]), where the negative relation is to the other indicators in
the same sub-pillar. Whether a sign-changing series would trigger it is not specified in the Report and
is read here as an inference. The mechanism is not peculiar to this index, as the standard reference on
composite construction holds that under principal components weighting corrects for overlapping
information rather than measuring importance, and that where indicators are not correlated weights
cannot be estimated by the method at all (Nardo et al., 2008, §6.1) — a variable that is both
low-variance and liable to correlate negatively with its neighbours is therefore poorly served by
loading-derived weights on the method's own terms. Net extent change belongs
beside the composite as a reported companion series in a combined presentation, not inside it as a
forty-fifth indicator (Table 5).

## 8.4 What no account should supply

Half the environment pillar measures something no ocean account should be built to measure. The
renewable-energy sub-pillar carries 50.0% of the pillar (Juknis, Tabel 5, p. 94 [pdf 104]) and contains
no marine variable, being solar and hydropower capacity and generation throughout (Section 7.2).
Offshore energy accounting is a forward recommendation, and the point here is narrower: no existing
account can supply what that sub-pillar currently asks for, and building one to do so would be an
instrument error. The article's coverage claim stops at that boundary, and stopping is what makes it
credible. Everything the mapping and the forward register recommend rests on tag (b) pilot compilation,
and the case for it is a production-process case rather than a weight case.

## 8.5 Limitations of this study

Eight limitations follow. The first bears on the argument itself and the rest on specific figures, and
they are ordered accordingly rather than by the sequence in which they arose.

The first is the counter-instance this article's own evidence supplies to its own positive claim. The
only compiled Indonesian account has produced two official extent series for the same pilot, the same
three ecosystems and the same two years, and they disagree not by a constant offset but by a ratio
that moves across ecosystems and across the accounting window, so the two documents disagree on the
change as well as on the level, MMAF recording a coral loss of 12.00 ha against a BPS loss of 18.26 ha
(Section 4.2). The pilot's own reporting compounds it, the narrative giving live hard coral cover of
16.99% for 2021 against 18.36% in the report's table on the same page (Section 5.2). Change is the
quantity a balancing identity exists to report, and provenance is what this article claims accounts
buy, so a divergence of this kind is the strongest available evidence against that claim and is
interrogated rather than set aside (Section 9.1). No reconciliation is published and none is attempted
here `[EVIDENCE GAP]`.

Second, the printed aggregation formulae are illegible in the PDF text layer at Juknis pp. 64–65 and
Report pp. 31–32. They are recorded here from their variable definitions, which are unambiguous about
Z-score inputs and first-principal-component loading weights, so what is reconstructed is the notation,
and the exact summation form at medium-high confidence rather than the method. Third, whether Tabel 4
to Tabel 6's percentages are raw loadings or normalised contributions is not stated anywhere, and every
derived figure in this article inherits that, including 7.64% `[CONTESTED DENOMINATOR]`, 13.7%, 5.15%
and 2.33% `[ASSUMPTION]`. The ambiguity is bounded rather than open, because the test is to renormalise
each sub-pillar's printed variable weights to sum to 100 and recompute. In the resource-quality
sub-pillar the nine printed weights already sum to exactly 100.0, so renormalisation leaves 18.45%,
7.64%, 3.58% and 2.33% unchanged to the precision the handbook prints, and the residual uncertainty is
the half-unit rounding of one-decimal inputs, which moves 7.64% by less than 0.1 percentage points. In
the economy pillar the five printed sub-pillar weights sum to 96.0 rather than 100, the residual being
the technology sub-pillar, which is not printed and is recovered here as 4.11%. Renormalising over the
five printed weights alone would instead give about 27.2% for the four Tier B sub-pillars and about
5.37% for governance, and Gambar 11's independently printed integers of 26 and 5 favour the reading
adopted here, so the alternative is reported as an upper bound of about one percentage point rather
than carried. Fourth, the per-indicator imputation record is
unpublished, so which indicators were predicted, for which provinces, by which model and at what error
are all unknown, which is the largest single unknown among the figure-level limitations because it
determines how much of any published provincial value is measurement and how much is prediction.
Fifth, two items sit off the document: Annex 3's per-province analysis is behind a short link, and the
normalised factor of 0.6819 is printed under four provincial tables without formula or derivation
(Table 1).

Sixth, this study engaged no Indonesian agency, by project constraint, so the limitations above are
conditions to live with rather than future work, and that distinction matters for how a reader should
weigh them. Seventh, the mapping is an assessment against stated criteria rather than an independently
verifiable quantity, which is why the score and the exclusion ground for each of the 44 register rows
are deposited in full rather than summarised, so that a reader may reject a row and recompute. Eighth,
compilation cost is absent from the public record at every site, so the conditions precedent are not
costed and could not be.


# 9. Discussion: gains from compilation

## 9.1 What Tier A compilation changes

Three results carry the argument. Examining the production system beneath the index found defects that
are properties of provenance rather than of arithmetic, being an imputation framework whose
per-indicator application is unpublished, a rescaling factor printed without derivation, and two
official extent series for the one compiled Indonesian account that cannot be reconciled (Sections 2
and 4.2). Reading the register's verbatim constituent data items rather than its indicator names
established that three ecosystem condition indicators are definitionally matched to account output
rather than category-mismatched, that five of the 44 rows are the whole of what existing accounts
touch, and that the account types bearing on the larger share of the index are the least developed
(Sections 5 and 7). What follows from those is a production-process case and not a weight case, and it
is narrower than an efficiency claim, which the evidence assembled here would not support.

The register decomposes into 62 distinct constituent data
items across its 44 indicators (Section 5.1), of which the resource-quality sub-pillar accounts for 9
items across 9 indicators, and the five mapped indicators for 5 items. Three of those five, being indicators 19, 20
and 21, are cross-classifications of one extent-and-condition compilation, whilst indicator 24
corresponds to the account's structure without being produced by it and indicator 27 is a designation
total already held by its custodians rather than measured by an account (Section 5.3). The
consolidation is therefore three items into one production run, out of 62. That is a small share of the
register, and the argument is not that the count is large. It is that those three are among the items
the index presently has no way to show it measured rather than predicted.

The count is a surrogate for what a reader would want, being the number of separate source publications
a compiler must assemble, which the published record cannot yield, since no register row states a
producing agency and the only producer statement sits at activity level, naming KKP and BPS
collectively (SIRUSA kegiatan 94936).

What compilation adds is provenance of a specific kind. An account carries an opening stock, additions,
reductions and a closing stock over a defined spatial unit, which allows a provincial figure to be
revised on improved information and defended afterwards, where the published output offers an underived
rescaling factor beneath its regional tables (Table 1).

That claim has to be tested against the strongest counter-instance in this article's own evidence, and
the counter-instance is the account itself. Two official series for the Gili Matra pilot disagree at
the level and, because the ratio between them moves by ecosystem and by year, on the change as well,
so the one compiled Indonesian account has produced two closing stocks and two deltas for the same
site and the same window (Sections 4.2 and 8.5). Read one way this refutes the provenance claim
outright. Read against what the two documents actually are, it qualifies it in a specific place. The
two series are two parallel releases of the same underlying mapping layer, published in a presentation
and a report respectively, and neither is an account of the other, so what the record demonstrates is
that publishing account-shaped tables is not the same as running an account. The property being
claimed is not that compilation prevents divergence, which nothing in the standard promises, but that
a balancing identity gives divergence somewhere to appear, the opening stock, the additions, the
reductions and the closing stock being four entries that must reconcile and that a second compilation
must restate rather than replace. The Gili Matra case is evidence for that reading in a negative form,
as it is precisely because neither document was compiled under such an identity, and because no
revision rule required either to restate the other, that a 6.26 ha disagreement about coral change
could be published twice and reconciled nowhere. The reading is an argument from the account form
rather than a demonstration, since no Indonesian compilation has yet been revised under such a rule
and the claim therefore remains untested in Indonesia `[ASSUMPTION]`.

The objection that a well-documented statistical register could supply the same properties without any
account (Section 3) bears directly on that argument, and it is conceded in part. Standing unit and
classification definitions and documented vintage are acts of codification that a register discharges
unaided, and three of the five conditions precedent are codification rather than compilation
(Section 6). What does not reduce to a register is the residual named earlier, being the balancing
identity and the revision rule that alters the entries themselves. The residual is thin, and saying so
is more useful than defending a wider claim: for the three matched indicators, most of what an account
would contribute in the near term could be contributed by a published classification standard, and
what could not is the part that makes a change defensible rather than merely stated. A reader who
concludes that Indonesia should codify definitions before compiling anything has read the evidence
correctly, and that ordering is what Section 6.5 recommends.

A gain in
imputation is stated conditionally rather than claimed, as no baseline exists against which to measure
one (Section 2), so what can be claimed is that measured values for the three consolidated items would
be distinguishable from predicted ones.
Introducing an account-derived indicator would in any case relocate imputation rather than remove it,
which is why insertion should follow coverage (Section 6.5). That the distinction is worth publishing
is established outside Indonesia. The Ocean Health Index, a composite of comparable construction in the
same domain (Halpern et al., 2012), reports that 18.5% of its global score rests on gapfilled rather
than observed data, ranging from 1% to 43% across its ten goals and exceeding 60% for ten regions that
are predominantly small island territories, and its authors recommend that gapfilling be tracked from
the outset and presented in the same framework as the scores themselves (Frazier et al., 2016). The
IBEI publishes an imputation framework and no per-indicator record, so the comparison identifies a
reporting reform rather than a data programme, and one the index could adopt without any account being
compiled.

Everything downstream of the input is untouched. Account supply changes the value entering a slot, not
the slot, its weight or the aggregation rule, so the three-stage principal component procedure, the
loading-factor weights, the pillar shares and every limitation the index publishes about itself stand
exactly as they are (Section 8.1). What follows for the composite's stability is conditional on a
reading the handbook does not settle. If Tabel 4 to Tabel 6's percentages are loading-normalised
contributions, the mature route bears on about 7.64% of the composite `[CONTESTED DENOMINATOR]`,
against roughly 18.9% resting on account types compiled nowhere, and inserting account-derived values
at those rows would perturb the composite little, which cuts against the fear of destabilisation and
against any claim of a transformed environmental signal in equal measure. If they are raw loadings,
the share is not calculable from the published record and no statement about perturbation is available
in either direction. The renormalisation test bounds the difference between the two readings at under
a percentage point wherever it can be run (Section 8.5), and the conditional is stated rather than
collapsed because the test cannot be run on the pillar weights themselves. The
design findings this study reports are likewise unaffected by accounts and not repaired by them.

Two literatures bear on that conditional, and neither favours reading the printed percentages as
importance. The handbook that defines the method holds that under principal components weighting
"intervenes only to correct for overlapping information between two or more correlated indicators and
is not a measure of the theoretical importance of the associated indicator", and that under linear
aggregation weights are trade-offs rather than importance coefficients (Nardo et al., 2008, §6.1 and
p. 31 n. 4). Paruolo et al. (2013) then measure what each variable actually contributes to five
composites, the human development index among them, and find that because socio-economic variables are
heteroscedastic and correlated, relative nominal weights hardly ever match relative effective
importance. The IBEI is a composite over correlated provincial socio-economic variables with weights
taken from first-component loadings, which is the configuration in which that divergence was found. The
consequence here is specific and is not evaded, as every share this article reports, the 7.64%, the
18.9% and the 26.1% alike, is a nominal share of printed weight rather than a measure of effective
influence, and the inversion is a comparison of nominal shares. Becker et al. (2017) supply the test
that would settle it, being a correlation-ratio measure of each variable's importance with confidence
intervals, and it cannot be run here because it requires the provincial variable matrix beneath the
index, which is unpublished `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. What survives the objection is the production-process
case, which never rested on the shares, and what does not survive unqualified is any reading of the
inversion as a statement about influence on a provincial score.

## 9.2 Loose coupling as architecture

Where substitution is not warranted, and on the published maturity evidence it is not, the arrangement
that delivers the gain is loose coupling. It has three requirements, being (1) shared indicator definitions at the
cover-class thresholds the Juknis already prints, so an account can tabulate to the index's own
definition, with the unit and denominator rules the register leaves open settled with its custodian
(Section 5.4), (2) a shared spatial unit that aggregates to the province, and (3)
parallel publication of the account alongside the index. It is defined as much by what it does not
require, being no institutional transfer, no change to the index's data pipeline, and no dependency of
a statutory index on an experimental compilation.

Both halves have a route in the standard. Parallel publication is a combined presentation
(United Nations et al., 2024, paras 14.56–14.57) and is the nearer term of the two, being a
presentational choice rather than a data gap, whilst sub-national compilation follows from the
accounting area being definable at a subnational administrative boundary (para. 2.12) and has been
demonstrated at that scale elsewhere (Gacutan et al., 2022), though nowhere in Indonesia beyond the
pilot (Section 4.1). Timing governs adoption either way (Section 6.1), and loose coupling has the
property that a compilation cycle missing the window fails without the index failing with it.

The institutional division holds at the point of recommendation, where it is most at risk. BAPPENAS is
*pengampu* of the calculation whilst the constituent data are provincial (Juknis, p. 68 [pdf 78]), so
nothing here asks either institution to take on the other's mandate. The arrangement would also have
to be settled by whichever body now holds the coordinating function, the team constituted under Kepmen
KP 44/2023 having had its term expire on 31 December 2023 with no renewal instrument located
(Sections 4.3 and 6.4), so an architecture requiring inter-agency coordination is being proposed
against a documentary record in which the standing coordination instrument has run its term.
Substitution becomes arguable when the conditions precedent are met (Section 6), and not before.

The evidence ceiling on all of this is one marine protected area, in one province, at one vintage,
carrying maturity tag (b) throughout (Section 4.1), and the architecture above is therefore a
specification of what would have to be true rather than a generalisation from a demonstrated national
capability. Two readings of the same record would lead elsewhere. Rinaldi et al. (2025) respond to the
weakness of Indonesian blue economy measurement by constructing an alternative index on a human
development index template, which treats the problem as one of index design rather than of the
production system beneath it, and on that reading the effort recommended here would be misdirected.
Wuwung et al. (2024) locate the constraint on Indonesian ocean policy in institutional fragmentation
across mandates, which would make an inter-agency supply arrangement the hardest rather than the
cheapest of the conditions precedent, against the ordering proposed here. A third reading questions the
target rather than the method. Ravallion (2012) defines a mashup index as a composite for which
existing theory and practice provides little or no guidance for its design, so that the producer is
essentially free to set an unusually large number of moving parts, and concludes that doubts remain
about the value added of such indices and their policy relevance relative to the dashboard alternative
of monitoring the components separately. On that reading the effort specified here is aimed at the
wrong object, and an account should be published alongside the components rather than routed into an
aggregate. The answer this record supports is statutory rather than methodological, as the IBEI is
scored for every province annually under Perpres 12/2025 (Section 1), so the dashboard alternative is
not in BAPPENAS's gift and the question of what supplies the composite is live whether or not an
aggregate is the instrument a statistician would have chosen. That is a reason to attend to the inputs
of this index, not a defence of aggregation in general. No source located by this study argues that
accounts cannot serve as statistical infrastructure for a composite index, which is the narrower
proposition advanced here.


# 10. Conclusion and follow-on

## 10.1 The single take-home

The single take-home is that ocean accounts are worth building for this index because of how the
handful of indicators they touch would be produced, and not because of how many indicators they could
fill, the share an account would itself supply being small on any reading of the published weights and
resting in part on a denominator the source documents do not settle (Section 9.1). The cost argument
below, the two follow-on studies and the statutory boundary are supporting rather than co-equal, and
each is presented as such.

## 10.2 Compilation cost, unpublished

No compilation cost appears anywhere in the record obtained for this study. There is no staff-day
figure, no budget line, no imagery cost and no field-survey cost for any Indonesian ocean account, at
any site `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. That absence, rather than any methodological obstacle in the conditions
precedent, is the binding constraint on acting on this study's recommendation, because a
province-by-province annual obligation is a scope decision before it is a method decision. This study
declines to estimate a cost, since an order-of-magnitude range would be read as a figure. The only
signal in the obtained record is computational rather than financial, in the grid-cell counts a single
bay generates at two resolutions (Rahayu et al., 2024), and processing burden for one bay is not a
compilation cost for a province.

## 10.3 Follow-on studies, and one risk

The claim that indicators 19, 20 and 21 are definitionally matched to account output, with the barrier
lying in compilation and geographic coverage rather than in measurement target, is empirically testable
today at exactly one site. Re-tabulating the Gili Matra compilation to the index's own 50% to 100% cut
would settle whether an account's extent and cover layers yield the register's class-share form at all,
and would generate the first real compilation-cost observation. Its limits are set by the record
itself, since the coral and seagrass cover behind that compilation was surveyed at stations rather than
mapped per cell (Section 5.2), the mangrove row is blocked on an undocumented unit conversion, and a
footprint of 2,980.84 ha supports no comparison against a published provincial value. Extending the
same tabulation to NTB as a whole is the step that would test provincial resolution, and it requires
the province-wide compilation that does not exist. Neither settles national coverage, the absent
spatial standard or the absent revision policy, and both carry an unknown on the index's side, since
whether the published values were themselves predicted is not recoverable.

The second is a compilation-guidance output rather than a research one. No published guidance sets out
how a sub-national marine ecosystem account should be compiled to serve a provincial indicator in an
Indonesian setting, and the material in Sections 2, 4 and 5 here is much of what such a document would
need. The sensible division is that the article supplies the analytical basis and the guidance document
the practitioner-facing output, each citing the other rather than duplicating it.

Both assume continued external technical assistance to the Indonesian programme. That is an assumption
rather than an established condition, no funding or programme-continuity document having been obtained
by this study `[EVIDENCE GAP]`. The cost study is the one item that becomes more valuable if such
assistance lapses, since it is what would let the decision be taken domestically.

What would falsify the argument is worth stating, because a paper of this shape is otherwise
structurally unfalsifiable. If a single-province compilation reproduces the published provincial value
no better than the current secondary-publication route, or does so at a cost per province that an
annual national cycle cannot bear, then the production-process case fails on its own terms.

Within the statutory boundary held throughout (Section 1), what this study describes is a capacity gap
under a statutory obligation, in a country that has demonstrated, at one experimental pilot site, one
instance of the production process the obligation would require at scale.


# Methods

**Design.** The study is a documentary analysis of two published specifications read against each
other: the Indonesia Blue Economy Index register as printed by its custodian, and the account
structures of the SEEA Ecosystem Accounting standard together with the specified sequence of national
ocean accounts. No sampling, no statistical inference and no hypothesis test is involved, and no
result reported here is an estimate with a sampling distribution. The equivalent burden is
reproducibility of a scoring rule and of an arithmetic chain, which the deposited materials discharge.

**Corpus.** A citation-anchored corpus of 199 chunks was built over the index's own published
documentation and released with this article, so that every quotation and every figure in the analysis
resolves to a numbered record rather than to a page range. Extraction was purposive rather than
exhaustive: the passages carrying the register, the weights, the calculation method, the sensitivity
analysis, the imputation framework, the provincial output and the cascading obligation were sliced from
each source at stated printed-page ranges, and nothing outside those ranges was chunked. The build,
dated 2026-08-14, comprises 106 chunks from the Juknis (BAPPENAS 2025a) over pp. 11–13, 63–66, 67–71,
93–96 and 101–104, 49 from the IBEI Report 2024 in its December 2025 revision (BAPPENAS 2025b) over
pp. 28–35, 64–66 and 67–70, 15 from Rinaldi et al. (2025) and 29 from the SIRUSA metadata for the
compilation activity and its indicator record (kegiatan 94936 and indikator 105371, both retrieved
2026-08-13). Each record carries a chunk identifier, its document and slice, the verbatim Indonesian
text, an English drafting gloss used nowhere as a quotation source, the printed page with the PDF page
in brackets, a content type, a maturity tag and a flag marking whether any part of the record was
reconstructed rather than read directly, which 17 of the 199 records carry. Chunks in conflict with one
another are recorded as conflicts rather than reconciled, and the four-against-three pillar contradiction
is held as one such record.

Account-side evidence sits outside that corpus and was read directly: the three MMAF 2022 reports
including the Gili Matra pilot study, the BPS materials prepared for the SEEA workshops, Rahayu et al.
(2024), Kepmen KP 44/2023 and the GOAP country page (retrieved 2026-08-13). All source documents carry
clean text layers and no optical character recognition was applied anywhere, so the two passages
recorded as illegible (Section 8.5) are defects of the published files rather than of extraction.
Retrieval and assembly ran from 11 to 16 August 2026. Alongside the corpus, a join table reproduces all
44 register rows with each row's *Data Pembentuk Indikator* constituent data items verbatim,
transcribed from Juknis Tabel 1, pp. 11–13 [pdf 21–23], and totalling 62 constituent data items. That
join table, and not the running text of the handbook, is the object the membership rule was applied to,
which is what makes the scoring reproducible from the deposit alone.

**Search protocol.** Searching was bilingual and was run over the document set named above rather than
over a statistical catalogue. The Indonesian terms used were *indeks ekonomi biru*, *neraca laut*,
*akun laut*, *neraca ekosistem*, *unit spasial dasar laut*, *peta jalan ekonomi biru* and *Satu Data
Indonesia*, together with their English equivalents, with no date restriction. No dedicated search of
the BPS statistical catalogue or of the Satu Data Indonesia portal was undertaken, and by project
constraint no Indonesian agency was approached for data, interview or correspondence. Every absence
claim in the article is therefore stated as unfound within the named set and is not a claim about the
Indonesian statistical system.

**Unit of analysis and the membership rule.** The unit is the register row, of which there are 44, and
the object tested is the row's constituent data items rather than its title, on the Indonesian wording.
Each row was graded on one of three grounds: *matched*, where a constituent data item names a quantity
that a defined SEEA Ecosystem Accounting output contains, *partial*, where account output supplies a
component of the quantity but not the quantity itself, and *provenance question*, where account output
bears on the row without supplying it. Tier A is the union of the three grades and has five members,
being indicators 19, 20 and 21 matched, 24 partial and 27 a provenance question. All remaining 39 rows
are graded none. The grade and the ground for every row, with the constituent data items reproduced,
are deposited as Supplementary Table S1 so that a reader may reject a row and recompute. The rule was
applied item by item rather than row by row, so a row carrying several constituent data items takes the
highest grade any one of its items attains, which is why the 62 items and not the 44 rows are the
tested population. Two decisions were fixed before scoring and held throughout: that indicator titles
are not evidence of what a row requires, titles having produced the predecessor figures the analysis
rejects, and that a definitional match carries no claim about whether anything has been compiled, the
two axes being reported separately at every use. Grading was performed by the authors against those
criteria, not blind, with no second coder and no adjudication protocol, which bounds what the exercise
can claim and is recorded among the limitations (Section 8.5). Replication of the scoring therefore
requires the deposited join table, the rule above and the SEEA Ecosystem Accounting output structures,
and requires nothing this study holds privately.

**The repeatability test.** Membership in Tier A is a definitional property and carries no claim about
compilation. The separate operative test applied to the account side is repeatability into a dated
window rather than demonstrated capability, since the obligation is annual and can receive only what an
upstream producer has already released at the required vintage (Section 6.1). Maturity is tagged on
every claim that something exists, as (a) published official statistics, (b) experimental or pilot
compilation, (c) methodology under development, or (d) announced but not started, and the tag describes
the datum rather than the document reporting it.

**Weight arithmetic.** Weights are taken from the Juknis as printed: pillar weights from Tabel 4, p. 94
[pdf 104], the environment pillar's sub-pillar split from Tabel 5, p. 94 [pdf 104], variable weights
from Tabel 6, pp. 95–96 [pdf 105–106], and the sub-pillar shares of Gambar 11, p. 93 [pdf 103], used as
an independent integer cross-check. Shares of the composite are products of those published
percentages, carried to two decimal places from one-decimal inputs and reported in prose to one decimal
with 'about'. The technology sub-pillar's weight is not printed and is recovered as the residual of the
economy pillar, at 4.11%. All weight arithmetic follows the three-pillar structure of Tabel 4, Tabel 5
and Gambar 11, on the ground that weights are published only there, whilst register rows keep their
Tabel 1 labels. The two structures disagree over indicators 15 to 18, and figures computed over the
pillars across which those rows move are identified as structure-dependent at each use (Section 2.3).

**Sensitivity to the weight ambiguity.** The handbook does not state whether the percentages in Tabel 4
to Tabel 6 are raw first-principal-component loadings or normalised contributions. The test applied is
to renormalise each sub-pillar's printed variable weights to sum to 100 and recompute. In the
resource-quality sub-pillar the nine printed weights already sum to exactly 100.0, so the renormalised
and printed readings coincide and 18.45%, 7.64%, 3.58% and 2.33% are unchanged to the precision
printed, with a residual half-unit rounding uncertainty moving 7.64% by under 0.1 percentage points. In
the economy pillar the five printed sub-pillar weights sum to 96.0, and renormalising over those five
alone would give about 27.2% for the four Tier B sub-pillars and about 5.37% for governance against the
26.1% and 5.15% adopted here, an upper bound of roughly one percentage point which Gambar 11's printed
integers of 26 and 5 do not support. The test cannot be run on the pillar weights themselves, which
already sum to 100.0 as printed.

**Reporting conventions.** Indonesian decimal commas are preserved inside quotation and rendered as
points in prose. Quotations are from the Indonesian source text. Three inline flags mark the evidential
status of statements: `[EVIDENCE GAP]` where a required source was not found, `[ASSUMPTION]` where the
argument depends on something unconfirmed, and `[CONTESTED DENOMINATOR]` on the mature-route share of
7.64% and on figures derived from it, recording that the share measures what these account types bear
on rather than what an account would supply (Section 5.4). Conflicting sources are reported as
conflicts and are not averaged or adjudicated.
