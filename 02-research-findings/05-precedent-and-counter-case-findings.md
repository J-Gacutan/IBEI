# **Strategic Assessment of Integrating Ocean Accounts into the Indonesia Blue Economy Index**

## **1\. Executive Summary and Contextual Background**

The Government of Indonesia has prioritized the transition toward a sustainable maritime paradigm, operationalizing this vision through two distinct but parallel measurement frameworks. The first is the Indonesia Blue Economy Index (IBEI), a composite index developed by the Ministry of National Development Planning (Bappenas)1. The IBEI is designed to monitor and benchmark the progress of blue economy development across all 38 Indonesian provinces. It utilizes a framework comprising economic, social, and environmental pillars, supported by governance and technology enablers2. In its 2022 baseline, the national IBEI score was reported at 40.4 out of 100, with a target of reaching 80 by the nation's centenary in 20451. The second framework consists of formal Ocean Accounts, compiled collaboratively by the Ministry of Marine Affairs and Fisheries (MMAF) and Statistics Indonesia (BPS) under the guidelines of the System of Environmental-Economic Accounting Ecosystem Accounting (SEEA EA) and the Global Ocean Accounts Partnership (GOAP)5. Currently, these accounts exist at a pilot scale in specific sites such as Gili Matra and Saleh Bay, with plans for gradual national scaling5.  
A prevailing conceptual argument, articulated in prior domestic policy discussions, posits that the environmental pillar of the IBEI remains analytically fragile without the integration of account-derived stock and flow data. This argument suggests that Ocean Accounts should serve as the foundational statistical infrastructure feeding directly into the IBEI. The theoretical appeal of this integration is evident: it promises to replace imperfect proxy indicators with rigorous, spatially explicit data that can flag economic gains achieved solely through the liquidation of natural capital. However, this argument has not been subjected to rigorous stress-testing against international statistical precedent, mathematical aggregation theories, or the institutional realities of decentralized governance.  
This report delivers an exhaustive, expert-level evaluation of the proposed integration. By analyzing international precedents across the European Union, China, Canada, South Africa, and global frameworks like the Ocean Health Index and the World Bank's WAVES program, this analysis maps the empirical reality of linking environmental-economic accounts to policy indices. Furthermore, it constructs and evaluates a comprehensive counter-case, ultimately providing a definitive verdict on whether direct indicator substitution is a viable pathway for Indonesia, or whether alternative architectures—such as loose-coupling and parallel dashboarding—offer superior analytical fidelity and institutional durability.

## **2\. Part A: International Precedent and Mechanism Typology**

### **2.1 Accounts Feeding Composite Indices: Global Evidence**

A systematic review of global initiatives attempting to integrate natural capital, environmental-economic, or ocean accounts into composite indices yields a highly significant finding: there is virtually no durable international precedent for the direct substitution of raw, multi-dimensional SEEA account outputs into a sub-national, unitless composite scorecard comparable to the IBEI. While many nations compile advanced environmental accounts, statistical agencies and ministries universally favor macroeconomic aggregates, satellite accounting, or distinct dashboard ecosystems over direct composite integration.  
To evaluate the landscape, it is necessary to examine the specific architectures employed by leading nations and institutions. ### **2.2 Mechanism Typology and Durability**

The analysis of international cases reveals four distinct integration mechanisms utilized by governments and international bodies to link environmental data with economic policy. The durability and statistical validity of these mechanisms vary significantly.  
The first mechanism is **Dashboard Integration**, best exemplified by the European Union’s Blue Economy Observatory and Australia's Environmental-Economic Accounts dashboard16. Under this typology, account data does not feed a single composite score. Instead, the data populates a shared digital infrastructure where users can view traditional economic metrics (such as Gross Value Added and employment) side-by-side with environmental indicators (such as marine living resource health or coastal pollution). This mechanism proves highly durable because it preserves the dimensional integrity of the data; an economist can analyze the financial metrics while an ecologist can independently verify the physical extent accounts without the two domains distorting one another34.  
The second mechanism is **Macroeconomic Aggregation**, prominently featuring the World Bank’s Adjusted Net Savings (ANS) and China’s Gross Ecosystem Product (GEP)10. This approach monetizes environmental degradation or ecosystem services and directly adds or subtracts them from a monolithic economic aggregate like Gross National Income (GNI). While durable at the global reporting level, this mechanism operates strictly within the boundaries of traditional national accounting rules (SNA). It does not attempt to merge social indicators, governance metrics, and environmental data into a unitless scorecard, maintaining a strict adherence to monetary valuation24.  
The third mechanism is **Satellite Accounting**, utilized effectively by South Africa, Portugal, and the United Kingdom29. In this model, the statistical office reorganizes existing national accounts using SEEA frameworks to isolate the "ocean economy" as a distinct, measurable sector. The outputs remain standard statistical tables—such as supply and use matrices—rather than arbitrary scores. This mechanism shows exceptional durability because it relies on certified, SNA-consistent methodologies that statistical offices already trust and understand29.  
The fourth mechanism is **Direct Indicator Substitution into Composite Indices**, which mirrors the proposed pathway for the IBEI. In this scenario, highly structured, multi-dimensional account data (such as the physical extent of mangroves or the condition of coral reefs) is normalized into a dimensionless sub-score (e.g., 0 to 100\) and averaged with disparate socio-economic data to create a single ranking. Crucially, the precedent review reveals that this mechanism is functionally non-existent in robust official statistics. No national statistical office currently maintains this mechanism for sub-national political benchmarking due to profound methodological vulnerabilities and the loss of analytical interpretability12.

### **2.3 Failure Cases: Stalled Integrations and Abandoned Metrics**

Evaluating policy failures is paramount, as successful launches are heavily publicized while methodological collapses are often buried in technical closeout reports. The friction between rigorous environmental accounting and the demands of political performance metrics has caused several high-profile integration attempts to stall.  
The most instructive failure case for Indonesia is China's "Green GDP" initiative (2004–2009). The Chinese central government, recognizing the severe ecological costs of its rapid economic expansion, attempted to introduce an index that deducted the cost of environmental damage and resource depletion from traditional GDP12. The initial reports indicated that pollution cost the country approximately 3% of its economic output13. However, the integration rapidly collapsed due to intense local political resistance. Provincial governors and mayors, whose political promotions were inextricably linked to traditional GDP growth, rebelled against an environmentally adjusted score that penalized their administrative performance. Several provinces lobbied the central government to cancel the metric, viewing it as an unfair limitation on their economic mandates13. Furthermore, severe institutional turf wars erupted between the State Environmental Protection Administration (SEPA), which advocated for the metric, and the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS), which argued that the valuation of environmental damage was too scientifically immature and legally subjective to be mixed with standardized national accounts13. The initiative was quietly suspended, though China later pivoted to the mathematically separate and less politically punitive Gross Ecosystem Product (GEP) framework11.  
Similarly, the World Bank’s Wealth Accounting and the Valuation of Ecosystem Services (WAVES) program encountered significant hurdles. While WAVES successfully generated robust natural capital accounts in developing nations such as Rwanda, Botswana, Costa Rica, and the Philippines, the program's official closeout reports noted severe friction when attempting to transition from simply publishing accounts to embedding them into hard policy indices27. While some nations successfully used the underlying data for specific sectoral planning—such as water allocation modeling or forestry management—attempts to replace headline macroeconomic indices were largely abandoned. The primary barriers were chronic data gaps, the compilation lag inherent in environmental telemetry, and the inability of policy-makers to interpret complex, multidimensional ecosystem tables when compressed into broad scorecards27.  
These cases serve as a direct warning for Indonesia: if the IBEI is utilized to evaluate the performance of Indonesia's 38 Governors or 514 Regents and Mayors, injecting highly sensitive Ocean Account data that penalizes them for natural capital depletion will inevitably trigger the exact political and institutional resistance observed in China12.

### **2.4 Sub-national Comparability and Uneven Coverage**

The defining characteristic of the IBEI is its capacity for provincial comparison, allowing the central government to benchmark regions against one another2. However, Indonesia's Ocean Accounts currently exist primarily in limited pilot sites, such as Gili Matra and Saleh Bay, rather than as a continuous national dataset5.  
International precedent strictly advises against building sub-national composite indices on uneven spatial data. When Statistics Canada compiles products like the Human Landscape Modification Index (HLMI), it relies strictly on comprehensive, national-level satellite telemetry and contiguous land-cover registers. This ensures that every pixel of the country is measured using identical methodologies, guaranteeing true comparability18.  
Conversely, when globally applied composites like the Ocean Health Index (OHI) encounter missing data at sub-national or regional levels, they must employ aggressive gap-filling methodologies. While the OHI is a durable academic tool, its creators explicitly acknowledge that regions requiring heavy gap-filling produce highly uncertain scores, which severely undermines the transparency and actionable credibility of the index for strict policy enforcement22. If Indonesia were to score West Nusa Tenggara using precise, high-resolution SEEA EA data derived from Saleh Bay, while simultaneously scoring Maluku using national-average proxies or gap-filled estimates, the resulting IBEI ranking would cease to measure true blue economy performance. Instead, it would merely map data availability, a practice no credible national statistical office engages in for headline indices.

### **2.5 Transferability to the Indonesian Context**

Indonesia’s unique geopolitical and administrative reality—an archipelagic state with 38 decentralized provinces, an extensive marine area, pilot-scale ocean account coverage, and a highly capable central statistical office (BPS) navigating complex inter-ministerial dynamics—dictates how international precedents can be applied.  
The transferability of the examined cases ranks as follows:

> 1. **China's Green GDP (High Transferability of Cautionary Lessons):** China shares a decentralized administrative structure where provincial leaders compete intensely on economic metrics. The failure of Green GDP demonstrates that exposing regional leaders to an environmental penalty index without perfect, indisputable, and universally agreed-upon data will lead to the tool's political rejection13.  
> 2. **South Africa's OESA (High Transferability of Mechanism):** By mapping ocean value chains (such as marine tourism and offshore extraction) into pure satellite accounts, South Africa avoids the indexing problem entirely29. This provides BPS and MMAF with a highly transferable roadmap for publishing objective, unassailable data without engaging in subjective scoring.  
> 3. **EU Blue Economy Observatory (High Transferability of Architecture):** The EU’s unified web dashboard offers a perfect architectural model. Bappenas can display the IBEI score on one interface, while MMAF and BPS display spatial Ocean Accounts adjacent to it, allowing the tools to complement rather than corrupt one another34.  
> 4. **Ocean Health Index (Low Transferability):** While theoretically appealing, the OHI’s reliance on academic gap-filling and algorithmic weighting20 is unsuitable for a sovereign statistical office that requires defensible, standardized data to mandate provincial policy.

### **2.6 Methodological Guidance on Integration**

Authoritative methodological bodies explicitly warn against the careless integration of highly structured accounting data into simplified composite indices. The UN *System of Environmental-Economic Accounting Ecosystem Accounting* (SEEA EA) framework manual dedicates significant discussion to the use of indicators and indices. It explicitly notes that while composite indices facilitate ease of communication, *"reducing the number of indicators by condensing information also runs the risk of misinterpretation because users are not always aware of the scope and limitations of the index methodology... The advantages of ease of communication and concise presentation of a composite index must thus be balanced against the problem of choosing units and weights required for aggregation"*39.  
Furthermore, standard frameworks governing index construction, such as the OECD/JRC *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators*, establish that robust composite indices require mathematical normalization (e.g., min-max scaling, z-scores) across the entire dataset to render disparate units comparable. Mathematical normalization is functionally impossible if the underlying dataset is comprised of highly precise, localized pilot data mixed with broad national proxies. The variance introduced by combining differing data vintages and collection methodologies fundamentally destroys the standard deviation required to calculate a valid z-score, rendering the resulting index statistically meaningless and highly vulnerable to critique.

## **3\. Part B: The Counter-Case**

The original concept note driving this inquiry suggests that the IBEI's environmental pillar is analytically deficient without account-derived stock and flow data, concluding that Ocean Accounts must serve as the statistical infrastructure feeding the index. To rigorously stress-test this hypothesis, it is necessary to construct the strongest possible objection to this integration in good faith (steel-manning). This requires evaluating whether the proposed integration is theoretically sound, statistically viable, and politically survivable.

### **3.1 Constructing the Strongest Objection**

An independent, critical statistician evaluating the proposal to feed Ocean Accounts into the IBEI would raise the following nine decisive arguments against integration:  
**1\. Instrument Mismatch (Category Error)** A composite index and an environmental-economic account are fundamentally distinct epistemological instruments designed for entirely different purposes. The IBEI exists to reduce complexity for high-level political communication; it compresses diverse economic, social, and environmental realities into a single, digestible ranking (e.g., a score of 49.58) to benchmark provincial progress3. Conversely, Ocean Accounts (based on SEEA EA) exist precisely to *preserve* analytical structure and complexity, tracking dynamic stocks and flows across physical and monetary dimensions to map intricate ecosystem dependencies6. Compressing an exhaustive, multidimensional ecosystem account into a normalized 0–100 index sub-score destroys the exact structural granularity that makes the account valuable in the first place. The integration serves neither purpose: the index becomes too complex and delayed to easily communicate, and the account is stripped of its diagnostic utility.  
**2\. Compensatory Aggregation Defeats the Core Purpose** The IBEI utilizes an additive composite structure combining economic, social, and environmental pillars2. In an additive index, variables are perfectly compensable, meaning a high score in one pillar can mathematically erase a low score in another. If a province actively destroys 10,000 hectares of critical mangrove habitat to construct a highly lucrative blue-infrastructure port, its Ocean Account condition score will crater, but its economic pillar score will maximize. The arithmetic mean allows the economic gain to completely offset the ecological loss, potentially resulting in a net *increase* in the province's overall IBEI score. SEEA accounts are designed precisely to flag this type of unsustainable natural capital liquidation6. Placing SEEA data inside an additive index mathematically guarantees that ecological depletion will be masked by short-term economic growth, directly violating the fundamental premise of sustainable blue economy monitoring.  
**3\. Coverage Insufficiency** The IBEI is mandated to evaluate all 38 Indonesian provinces to facilitate national benchmarking2. Currently, Indonesia’s Ocean Accounts exist only at a pilot scale (e.g., Gili Matra, Saleh Bay)5. An index cannot function legitimately with 5% empirical accounting data and 95% proxy data. If Bappenas attempts to score all provinces by extrapolating pilot data, the statistical variance will result in total incomparability across regions. Conversely, if they only score the provinces with complete accounts, the IBEI ceases to function as a national policy tool. Partial adoption creates an incomparability paradigm that is statistically worse than the imperfect proxies it aims to replace22.  
**4\. Interpretability Loss** Ocean Accounts utilize a highly mixed matrix of measures: physical extent (hectares), condition indices (water quality parameters), physical flows (tons of fish harvested), and monetary asset valuations38. Attempting to normalize mixed stock (long-term assets) and flow (annual production) variables into a single, flattened environmental pillar creates severe interpretability issues. If a province's environmental score falls by five points, a policymaker reading the index will have no way of knowing whether the drop occurred because an ecosystem permanently shrank (a catastrophic stock loss) or because sustainable harvesting quotas were temporarily reduced (a manageable flow change). This renders the index harder to read and easier to game, rather than more informative.  
**5\. Lag Incompatibility** To align with the National Medium-Term Development Plan (RPJMN) cycle, the IBEI requires highly current data (T-1 year) to ensure relevant provincial benchmarking47. However, SEEA Ocean Accounts require the harmonization of vast geospatial, ecological, and economic datasets. The intense data verification processes routinely result in compilation lags of two to three years (T-2 or T-3)14. Integrating the two frameworks means the IBEI's environmental pillar will perpetually reflect stale ecological realities compared to its real-time economic pillar, fundamentally misinforming current-year policy interventions.  
**6\. Institutional Cost and Turf Wars** Integrating the frameworks requires seamless, ongoing data transfer and methodological consensus between Bappenas (the index owner), MMAF (the sectoral data holder), and BPS (the statistical authority). Forcing these distinct institutions to agree on subjective indicator weightings, environmental valuation methodologies, and complex normalization algorithms will consume vast amounts of political capital. As demonstrated by the failure of China's Green GDP, inter-ministerial conflict over the "correct" way to penalize economic growth for environmental damage can stall both programs indefinitely, shifting focus from environmental protection to bureaucratic negotiation13.  
**7\. Non-Overlapping Decision Needs** The two frameworks answer fundamentally different questions for entirely different audiences. The IBEI answers the high-level political question: *"Which province is generally improving its maritime economy relative to the national target?"*3. In contrast, Ocean Accounts answer highly specific management questions: *"What is the exact spatial condition and economic valuation of the seagrass beds in Saleh Bay, and how does the degradation of that specific asset impact local artisanal fisheries?"*8. Forcing a linkage assumes that the political audience benchmarking governors and the technical audience managing marine spatial plans are identical. They are not, and merging the tools constitutes a category error.  
**8\. Sequencing Objection** Even if full integration is theoretically desirable by the 2045 centenary, attempting it in the near term is premature. Integrating an immature, pilot-scale accounting framework into a highly visible national index guarantees that the first iteration will be heavily criticized by regional governments for data gaps and inaccuracies. This early, highly public failure risks discrediting the entire concept of Ocean Accounting in the eyes of Indonesian policymakers before national scaling can even gain traction.  
**9\. Cost-Benefit Failure** The financial and logistical cost of artificially accelerating national Ocean Account coverage simply to supply a few normalized indicators for a composite index is a severe misallocation of the national statistical budget. SEEA data is highly expensive and complex to compile. Its true value lies in supporting localized Marine Spatial Planning, complex ecosystem valuation, and specific policy testing—not in serving as a highly compressed data feeder for a high-level composite score.

### **3.2 Assessment of the Counter-Case**

Evaluating the counter-case rigorously reveals that the objections are not merely pessimistic or academic; they are structurally fatal to the concept of direct integration.

* **Decisive Arguments (Fatal to Integration):** The arguments regarding *Instrument Mismatch* and *Compensatory Aggregation* are insurmountable. Mathematics dictates that it is impossible to preserve the "strong sustainability" warning signals inherent in SEEA accounts if they are embedded within an additive index where economic gains can mathematically erase environmental losses. Similarly, the *Coverage Insufficiency* argument is currently fatal; an index cannot survive political scrutiny if it relies on 95% gap-filled proxy data22.  
* **Timing Arguments:** The *Lag Incompatibility* and the *Sequencing Objection* are issues of timing rather than absolute merit. If, in 2040, BPS achieves real-time, automated spatial telemetry for all 38 provinces, these objections dissolve. However, they remain binding constraints that prohibit integration for the next decade.  
* **Surmountable Arguments:** The *Institutional Cost* and *Non-Overlapping Decision Needs* could theoretically be overcome with strong Presidential mandates and robust inter-ministerial Memorandums of Understanding.

**Settling the Evidence:** To defeat the compensatory aggregation argument, proponents of integration would have to entirely redesign the IBEI into a non-compensatory geometric index or a multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) framework. To defeat the coverage argument, BPS and MMAF would have to produce a timeline proving full, wall-to-wall 38-province SEEA-EA ocean account coverage within the current RPJMN cycle. Neither of these redesigns is currently planned or feasible. Consequently, the counter-case holds definitively. Raw account outputs belong in a companion product, not inside the index itself.

### **3.3 The Null and Alternative Options**

Because direct integration is rejected based on methodological and political realities, two alternatives remain to be assessed:  
**The Null Option: Separate Frameworks** In this scenario, both frameworks develop entirely independently with no formal linkage. Bappenas continues to use available proxy data for the IBEI's environmental pillar (e.g., water quality sampling, percentage of Marine Protected Area coverage)1, while MMAF and BPS develop Ocean Accounts solely for localized spatial planning.

* *Assessment:* While politically safe, the null option represents a significant analytical loss. Under this scenario, the IBEI remains functionally blind to actual natural capital depreciation, allowing provinces to achieve high blue economy scores while quietly liquidating their underlying ecological assets. The concept note's assertion that an IBEI completely disconnected from SEEA is analytically fragile is fundamentally correct41.

**The Loose-Coupling Option: Shared Architecture, Separate Outputs** In this scenario, the IBEI and Ocean Accounts remain distinctly separate products, but they are fundamentally linked at the infrastructural and metadata levels:

> 1. **Shared Metadata and Definitions:** Both frameworks adopt the exact same definition of the "Blue Economy," explicitly based on SEEA and SNA industry classifications (e.g., aligning ISIC/KBLI codes for marine transport, fisheries, and tourism)29.  
> 2. **Shared Spatial Units:** Bappenas aligns the IBEI provincial and regency boundaries perfectly with the Basic Spatial Units (BSUs) utilized by the BPS Ocean Accounts50.  
> 3. **Parallel Publication (Dashboarding):** When Bappenas publishes the annual IBEI scores, the data is hosted on a unified "Blue Economy Observatory" dashboard, directly mirroring the successful European Union approach16. If a user or policymaker clicks on a specific province's IBEI score, a side-panel opens displaying that province's SEEA Ocean Account physical flow and extent tables.  
> 4. **No Indicator Substitution:** Crucially, SEEA data is *never* normalized into the IBEI score. It sits beside the score to provide immediate, unadulterated context.  
* *Assessment:* The Loose-Coupling option captures the vast majority of the analytical benefits envisioned by the original concept note at a fraction of the statistical and political risk. When the IBEI shows a province booming economically, the parallel Ocean Account immediately reveals if that boom is built on ecological depletion. Because the metrics do not mathematically interfere with one another, the political utility of the index and the diagnostic truth of the accounts are both preserved.

## **4\. Verdict**

On the balance of exhaustive international precedent and the undeniable mathematical realities of composite indexing, **direct integration of Ocean Accounts into the Indonesia Blue Economy Index (IBEI) is not worth doing.**  
The initial concept note's premise—that the IBEI is analytically weak without account-derived data—is theoretically accurate. However, its proposed solution—feeding account data directly into the index as normalized sub-scores—is a fundamental category error. Stripping the dimensionality from an Ocean Account to create a normalized score in an additive composite index destroys the analytical power of the SEEA framework. Furthermore, due to the compensatory nature of the IBEI, economic gains will simply mask environmental losses through mathematical averaging, neutralizing the very warning system the accounts were meant to provide. Compounding this is the insurmountable reality of spatial coverage; attempting to score 38 provinces using data extrapolated from a handful of pilot sites will mathematically compromise the IBEI, triggering the exact political and institutional resistance that destroyed China's Green GDP initiative.  
**Instead, Indonesia should forcefully pursue the Loose-Coupling option.** Bappenas, MMAF, and BPS should align their metadata, spatial classifications, and KBLI boundaries to ensure they are measuring the same universe. The outputs should then be presented side-by-side in a national Blue Economy Dashboard. The IBEI will serve its purpose as an accessible political benchmarking tool for the RPJMN cycle, while the Ocean Accounts will sit adjacent to it, serving as the rigorous, non-compensatory ledger of Indonesia's natural capital. This approach respects the distinct epistemological purposes of both instruments, aligning Indonesia with the safest, most durable statistical practices observed in the European Union, Canada, and South Africa.
